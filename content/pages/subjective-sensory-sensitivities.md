# Subjective Sensory Sensitivities

## Summary

Subjective sensory sensitivities refer to individual experiences of heightened discomfort, aversion, or distraction in response to sensory stimuli such as loud noises, bright lights, strong smells, or tactile sensations. Unlike sensory processing dysfunction (which focuses on nervous system atypicality), subjective sensitivities emphasize the lived experience and personal meaning of these sensations. They significantly impact daily functioning, relationships, and emotional wellbeing across neurodivergent and neurotypical populations.

## Key Claims

- **Sensory sensitivities limit functional and social participation.** Participants reported constraints on sleep, driving, shopping, public transport use, and other essential activities directly due to sensory challenges or indirectly through physical and emotional exhaustion. [[sensory-processing-dysfunction|Sensory sensitivities are effortful and exhausting]], requiring substantial energy to manage.

- **Sensory sensitivities create relational strain.** Individuals reported difficulty managing reactions in social contexts—including emotional volatility, avoidance of public spaces, and challenges in communicating needs to others—leading to miscommunication or perceived abnormality in personal relationships.

- **Exacerbating factors are multidimensional.** Sensory sensitivities are amplified by situational factors (environmental conditions, unpredictability, cumulative sensory load) and internal factors (emotional state, tiredness, anxiety, depression, physical health).

- **Coping strategies are diverse and context-dependent.** People employ multiple, often simultaneous strategies: limiting sensory input (avoidance, earplugs), using enjoyable sensory stimuli for distraction/masking, self-regulation (mindfulness, breathing, acceptance), social support, and control/prediction through routine.

- **Social context shapes expression and management of sensitivities.** Many individuals suppress their reactions in front of others due to concerns about how they appear or impact others, suggesting that [[double-empathy-problem|bidirectional understanding]] and environmental acceptance affect coping success.

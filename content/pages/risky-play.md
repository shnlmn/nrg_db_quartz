# Risky Play

## Summary

Play involving an element of risk or challenge—including height, speed, impact, loss of balance, or confrontation with physical or emotional threat. Risky play is recognized as developmentally essential for all children's physical, cognitive, emotional, and social growth, yet is often eliminated from school playgrounds due to institutional safety concerns.

## Developmental Importance

**Essential for brain and behavioral development** across neurotypes:
- Builds resilience and stress management capacity
- Develops confidence, autonomy, and sense of mastery
- Supports skill-building, coordination, and physical strength
- Enables meaningful peer interaction and social validation
- Promotes healthy self-assessment of personal capability and risk tolerance

Research (Hart, 1997; Gray, 2017) demonstrates that risky, unstructured play contributes to social-emotional growth; restricted access to challenging play is linked to rising anxiety and obesity.

## Institutional Barriers

**Safety-averse policies in schools** inadvertently exclude children by removing elements essential to development:

- **Removed elements** — Flying foxes, swings, sand pits, height, climbing opportunities eliminated due to liability concerns
- **No alternatives provided** — Risk-averse removal without replacement of challenge features
- **Over-supervision effects** — Adult anxiety restricts neurodivergent children's autonomy and reinforces dependence rather than building confidence
- **Differential impact** — Neurodivergent children disproportionately affected by lack of challenge and risk when combined with sensory barriers (overwhelming noise, poor design)

## Risky Play in Neurodivergent Development

From [[kelly-et-al-2025-inclusive-playgrounds|Kelly et al. 2025]]:

- **ND children actively seek risk elements** — Both neurodivergent and neurotypical children in co-design prioritized high-risk features (treehouses, hamster wheels, climbing) for fun, mastery, and peer validation
- **Essential vestibular/proprioceptive affordances** — High climbing and challenging features provide proprioceptive and vestibular feedback critical for neurodivergent self-regulation
- **Children as risk assessors** — ND children demonstrated capacity to identify safety concerns (firepit barriers) and suggest improvements, affirming their competence and capacity for risk judgment
- **Safety vs. development tradeoff** — Current policies optimize for zero-incident playgrounds at cost of children's physical and emotional development

## Reframing Risk

**Moving from risk aversion to responsive risk management**:

- **Controlled risk is beneficial** — Not elimination but thoughtful design supporting informed risk-taking within managed parameters
- **Model programs** — Queensland schools implementing "Loose Parts and Nature Play" programs with controlled risk (tree climbing licenses, firepits) report reduced negative behaviors and improved social-emotional outcomes
- **Neurodivergent self-regulation** — Risk-taking through vestibular play (spinning, height) supports neurobiological regulation essential for ND children's sustained attention and behavioral control

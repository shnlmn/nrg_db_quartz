# Sensory Experience in Architecture

## Summary

Sensory experience in architectural settings is the felt, embodied encounter with environmental stimuli through the senses and body. It involves active attention to how stimuli (light, sound, texture, heat, smell) are felt, distinct from passive stimulus presence.

## Key claims

- Sensory experience is not merely stimulus presence but emerges when humans actively focus on how stimuli are *felt* through senses and body [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- Most sensations go unattended unless they are notably pleasant or unpleasant; only extreme or salient sensations receive conscious attention and naming [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- A distinction exists between bodily sensations (pain, itches — localized to specific body parts) and bodily feelings (tiredness, sleepiness — diffuse, not body-part-specific) [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- Transitive sensations (warmth, pressure) can exist without a sentient being but may be experienced; intransitive sensations (pain) require a sentient being and represent bodily states [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- Contemporary built environments are characterized by "controlled homogeneity and predictability" of sensory qualities, which provides comfort but diminishes delight and sensory richness [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- Most empirical research on sensory experience focuses on single sensory domains; the interrelations between multi-sensory experiences are poorly understood [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]
- The interaction between senses during experience is significant: for example, odor can heighten or diminish the perception of other sensations (e.g., scent of seaweed affecting perception of sea breeze) [[raw/fpsyg-15-1303397.extracted.md]]

## Measurement approaches

- Visual analog scales with adjective pairs (e.g., signal vs. keynote sound) characterize sensory stimuli qualities
- Sensory notation (Lucas & Romice) uses adjective selection (rough/smooth, hard/soft, warm/cold) with priority ranking across senses
- RADES and similar techniques empirically capture what sensory experiences people actually notice and prioritize

## Open questions

- How do sensory deprivation and overload trade off in contemporary built environments?
- Which non-visual sensory qualities most impact place preference and wellbeing?
- How do multi-sensory experiences combine or interact — additively, synergistically, or through masking effects?
- Are there neurodivergent sensory processing phenotypes that predict differential environmental experience preferences?

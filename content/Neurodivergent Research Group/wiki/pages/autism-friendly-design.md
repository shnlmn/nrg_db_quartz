# Autism-Friendly Design

## Summary

Autism-friendly design is a specialized approach within [[neuro-inclusive-design]] that creates built environments (particularly indoor spaces and therapeutic settings) deliberately accommodating the sensory processing, social, and communication characteristics of autistic individuals. Mostafa's (2008, 2014) ASPECTSS™ Design Index is the most cited framework, though broader inclusive approaches extend beyond symptom-management toward celebration of autistic ways of being.

## Discourse Context

- Autism was long viewed as a monolithic, pathologized condition to be cured; this has shifted to a strength-based, identity-first position [[raw/Autism Design and an Architecture for All_ Designing for a Differently Abled World]]
- The peak cohort diagnosed during the early-2000s awareness surge is reaching adulthood; self-advocacy is an increasingly heard design stakeholder voice [[mostafa-2022-architecture-for-all]]
- ~1.5% of global population (~120 million) are on the autism spectrum; scale demands architectural response at a systemic, not niche, level [[mostafa-2022-architecture-for-all]]

## Key Design Guidelines

Research-informed autism-friendly design includes:

- **Sensory control** — Adjustable lighting, sound management, reduced visual complexity
- **Predictable spaces** — Clear wayfinding, unambiguous layouts, recognizable visual patterns
- **Refuge areas** — Safe, calm spaces away from overwhelming sensory input or social demands
- **Flexibility** — Multiple pathways, sensory options, and uses within spaces
- **Texture variety** — Diverse materials supporting tactile engagement
- **Color and pattern** — Considerations for visual processing and potential sensory sensitivities

## Existing Frameworks

**[[aspectss-design-index|Mostafa's ASPECTSS® Design Index]]** (developed 2013, American University in Cairo):
- Autism-specific design principles premised on the value of autistic insight
- Emphasis on school and therapeutic environments; primarily focused on children
- Three core spatial constructs: sensory seeking, sensory refuge, transition
- Exhibited at Venice Biennale 2021 and 2023; 2023 exhibition introduced [[sensory-decolonisation]] as conceptual thesis

**Other cited approaches:**
- Sachs (2011) — Therapies and treatments through design
- Gaines et al. (2016) — Broader autism spectrum disorder design
- Humphreys (2008) — Architecture and autism

## Policy and Sector Context

- No top-down legislation or codes currently require autism-inclusive design the way mobility, hearing, and vision impairments are mandated; it remains dependent on individual decision-makers' initiative [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]
- Sectors most active: higher education (driven by autistic student self-advocacy, e.g. DCU), tech workplaces (e.g. [[sap-autism-at-work|SAP Autism at Work, 2013]]), post-COVID workplace redesign [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]
- Most under-served sector: healthcare — autistic individuals have worse physical health outcomes with emerging evidence that healthcare space anxiety is a contributing cause (see [[autism-healthcare-outcomes]]) [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]
- "Good design is cheaper than bad design" — cost of inclusive modifications is offset by avoided losses in inefficiency, productivity, and retrofit [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]
- Post-occupancy scheduling and programming matter: if operations don't align with design intent, the building will fail regardless of build quality [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]

## Design Research Process

Standard human-centred design tools (focus groups, interviews, surveys, workshops) assume verbal communication, social group participation, and normative response formats — inadvertently excluding autistic participants [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]. Adaptations:

- Highly structured, predictable formats with minute-by-minute agendas (open-ended ambiguity increases anxiety)
- Regular check-ins; openness to alternative formats during the session
- Visual over verbal: sketching, drawing, marking-up, participant-transcribed responses
- Framing designers explicitly as learners: "I am here to hear from, learn from, and understand the autistic perspective"
- [[strength-based-approach-autism-design]] — autistic participants as expert informants, not charitable beneficiaries

## Limitations and Gaps

- Primarily addresses autistic children, not adults
- Focuses on indoor and therapeutic settings rather than public outdoor spaces
- Risk of essentializing autism as a monolithic profile ("if you've met one person with autism, you've met one person with autism")
- Lacks integration with other neurotypes (ADHD, dyslexia)
- Prescriptive approaches may not suit diverse individual needs
- No legislative mandate in most jurisdictions

## Cross-Framework Alignment and Neurotypical Benefits

Mostafa et al. (2023) [[mostafa-et-al-2023-aspectss-case-study]] mapped ASPECTSS and ASPECTSS 2.0 criteria against six external frameworks, finding alignment across all criteria with at least one other framework:

- **WELL Building Standard** Comfort Features 72–84 — overlaps on acoustical performance, thermal comfort, lighting, and olfactory qualities
- **Barrett et al. (2015)** — school design accounts for 16% of pupil learning; their constructs (light, ownership, flexibility, complexity, colour) map to ASPECTSS concepts
- **Lackney (2000)** 33 educational design principles — overlaps on safety, compartmentalization, escape, lighting, acoustics

This cross-framework convergence provides preliminary evidence that autism-specific design will not conflict with neurotypical users' comfort needs and may actively benefit them — supporting the [[design-for-autism-design-for-all]] thesis.

Additional evidence: transition pods at the Els for Autism school were spontaneously adopted by neurotypical staff (note-taking, preparation, semi-private conversation) and supported student-initiated social interactions — an unintended positive affordance emerging from sensory-focused design.

## Broader Context

Finnigan's (2024) [[sensory-responsive-environments-framework]] extends autism-friendly design principles to:

- Outdoor and public environments
- Multiple neurodivergent conditions simultaneously
- Multi-generational users (not just children)
- Non-prescriptive, context-sensitive frameworks
- [[design-from-sensorial-margins|Marginalized user representation]]

# Auditory Navigation Cues

## Summary

Sound cues that help people with visual impairment orient and navigate public spaces. Effective auditory cues include accessible pedestrian signals, echoes from buildings and objects, and landmark sounds. Auditory cues support wayfinding through providing directional information and confirming location.

## Key cues

### Audible Pedestrian Signals
- Beeps and chirps providing directional information for street crossing
- Allow people with visual impairment to know when to cross and maintain alignment in crosswalk [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Echoes and Reverberation
- Echoes from buildings and objects help travelers determine location and direction of travel
- When well-controlled (non-masking), echoes provide spatial information about building layout and proximity [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Landmark Sounds
- Fountain sounds, traffic noise, bus engines
- Provide directional orientation and serve as consistent wayfinding references [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Traffic Sounds
- Parallel traffic surge at street crossings indicates when street is clear and when to begin crossing
- Helps maintain directional alignment during crossing
- Can be problematic with hybrid/quiet vehicles [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

## Multi-sensory integration

People with visual impairment often use auditory cues alongside other sensory information to validate direction and determine next movement. Auditory information becomes most useful when:
- Clear and directional (not reverberating/masking)
- Distinct from surrounding noise
- Proximity-appropriate: loud sounds useful for distant wayfinding but problematic when close; quiet sounds dangerous when close but of little importance when far [[context-dependent-sensory-effectiveness]]

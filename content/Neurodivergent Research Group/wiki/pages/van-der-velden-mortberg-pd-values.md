# Van Der Velden Mortberg Pd Values

## Summary

van der Velden, M. & Mortberg, C. "Participatory Design and Design for Values." Book chapter building on the [[scandinavian-participatory-design|Scandinavian Participatory Design]] tradition. Presents six guiding principles of PD (equalising power relations, democratic practices, [[situation-based-action|situation-based action]], [[mutual-learning]], tools and techniques, alternative visions about technology), argues PD is a value-centred design approach where values emerge dynamically during the design process, contrasts PD with [[value-sensitive-design|Value Sensitive Design]], and discusses four participatory methods (card methods, mapping methods, [[future-workshop|future workshops]], [[participatory-prototyping]]) for exploring, engaging with, and materialising values.

## Key claims

- PD's methodology is based on genuine decision-making power of co-designers and incorporation of their values in the design process and its outcome; the core theme (Ehn 1988) is addressing the dialectics of tradition and transcendence — the tension between what is and what could be [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD is defined (Simonsen & Robertson 2012) as "a process of investigating, understanding, reflecting upon, establishing, developing, and supporting mutual learning between multiple participants in collective 'reflection-in-action'" [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Six guiding principles of PD: (1) equalising power relations; (2) democratic practices; (3) situation-based action; (4) [[mutual-learning]]; (5) tools and techniques; (6) alternative visions about technology [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD is a value-centred design approach because both politics and ethics want to realise values — "the idealized qualities or conditions in the world that people find good" (Brey 2010); PD projects do not frontload a fixed set of values but enable values to emerge and be co-created during the design process [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- The relationship of values to design is dynamic: values emerge during co-design whether or not designers look for them; values can change, evolve, and even conflict as the design process unfolds (Halloran et al. 2009) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Bjerknes & Bratteteig (1995) identify a turn from politics to ethics in Scandinavian PD: the system developer shifted from emancipator in a collective political process to professional facilitator with individual ethical responsibility; Steen (2013) calls this the "ethical turn" [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- [[value-sensitive-design|Value Sensitive Design]] (VSD) aims to advance a predetermined set of moral values (human welfare, privacy, autonomy, trust, etc.); Manders-Huits & Zimmer (2009) argue PD promotes functional/instrumental values while VSD promotes moral values — but this raises the question of whose values are advanced [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- The design process is understood as a "contact zone" (Haraway 2003; Pratt 1998) — a space for communication across irreducible differences and situated partial connections where different meanings of values meet and grapple with each other; consensus is agonistic and non-coercive (Mouffe 1993) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- [[use-before-use|Use-before-use]] (Ehn 2008): the central design challenge is that use of the designed object can only be envisioned or anticipated because there is no actual artefact yet; participatory methods engage co-designers in telling, making, and enacting use [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Genuine participation requires sharing decision-making power with co-designers; without this, participation becomes instrumental and exploitative (Keinonen 2010; Shapiro 2010); structural arrangements (resource allocation, method choice) also affect power to decide [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Silences may emerge during a design process and have implications for participants' actions; sensitivity is needed to pay attention to silences, as there is a tendency to neglect them (Karasti 2001; Mortberg & Stuedahl 2005) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Technology mediates behaviour through inscriptions (scripts for use); involving future users in the inscription process increases the likelihood that artefacts matter to them and are used as envisioned; but technology is multi-stable — it can have different stable meanings in different use contexts [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- In multicultural settings, PD's principle values of participation and democracy need to be explored in local context; participation is not necessarily associated with democracy in hierarchical societies (Winschiers-Theophilus et al. 2012) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Phronesis (practical wisdom) is an ethical root of PD: experience-based ethics oriented towards action, enabling imagination and emotions in the design process, and guiding it to serve the common good (Ehn & Badham 2002; Kanstrup & Christiansen 2006) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD has expanded from Scandinavian system development to urban planning, architecture, sustainable development, healthcare, and global settings; co-designers are now often expert users of the technology under design [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]

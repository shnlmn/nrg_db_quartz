# Focus Groups

## Summary

Focus groups are a qualitative research method using carefully planned group discussions to generate data about participants' perceptions on a defined topic in a permissive, non-threatening environment. They capitalize on interactive communication between participants, offering in-depth insights in a relatively short timeframe and are increasingly used in participatory research as a bridging strategy between scientific inquiry and community knowledge.

## Key claims

- Focus groups are particularly useful when complementing other data collection methods by providing in-depth insights in a relatively short amount of time [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- When conducting focus group research with community partners, Community Advisory Board members are most often involved in recruiting participants, moderating discussions, and disseminating findings to lay and community audiences [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Combining focus groups with other forms of participatory research (such as Community Advisory Board input) creates unique challenges, including overlapping recruitment pools between focus group participants and CAB members, and sometimes requires additional training for CAB members [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- On the [[participation-continuum]], traditional focus groups typically function at the "consult" level; however, they can be co-designed, co-facilitated, and collaboratively analyzed to reach "collaborate" level — the distinguishing feature is stakeholder power in decision-making, not the method itself [[raw/Participatory Research Methods – Choice Points in the Research Process _ Published in Journal of Participatory Research Methods.md|Vaughn & Jacquez]]
- For neurodivergent participants, [[reasonable-adjustments]] to focus groups are essential: offering multiple modes of participation (in-person and online); providing ground rules in both verbal and written formats; allowing breaks; stating questions both verbally and in writing (e.g., in chat for online groups); and permitting use of assistive software [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Online and in-person focus groups with neurodivergent participants should encourage (though not enforce) turning on cameras for online participation to provide a similar social experience across both formats [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Focus groups were the most frequently reported co-design activity in an umbrella review of 21 digital health intervention reviews, appearing in 17/21 reviews; interviews appeared in 14/21 and surveys in 11/21 [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Participants in digital health co-design reported difficulties freely talking to strangers in new settings including focus groups, highlighting the need for familiar environments and relationship-building [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Across a rapid overview of 23 health research co-design reviews, focus groups were identified (alongside interviews, surveys, and deliberative methods) as the most typical co-design activities; less frequent activities included citizens' juries, town meetings, the nominal group technique, and the Delphi technique [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]

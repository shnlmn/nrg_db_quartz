# Context-Dependent Sensory Effectiveness

## Summary

The effectiveness of sensory cues for people with visual impairment varies dramatically based on context. The same sensory stimulus can be helpful or hindering depending on proximity, intensity, the activity being performed, individual residual vision, and other contextual factors.

## Key contextual factors

### Proximity
Same sensory cue has opposite effects based on distance:
- **Loud noise** when close: problematic because it masks desired useful auditory information
- **Loud noise** when far: useful for directional orientation
- **Quiet sounds** (e.g., hybrid cars) when close: dangerous because cannot be easily heard
- **Quiet sounds** when far: of little importance [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Intensity and Quality
- **Sensory characteristics:** type, intensity, pitch, and tone all affect utility [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]
- **Loudness:** Loud traffic sounds helpful for determining walking directionality but problematic for identifying approaching bus
- **Echo and reverberation:** Helpful for spatial understanding when clear but hindering when distorting sound signals

### Individual Residual Vision
- **Lighting effects:** "Depending on the eye condition of the individuals, bright light helps some people and even dull lighting helps others" [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]
- Variability in what constitutes barrier vs. supporter

### Type and Nature of Activities
Different activities require different sensory information:
- **Directional orientation:** requires distant landmark sounds
- **Identification:** requires near, clear cues
- **Navigation in crowded space:** requires sound clarity
- **Leisure activities:** may prioritize different sensory experiences [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

## Implications for design

Context-dependence means:
1. No universal "good" or "bad" sensory features—functionality depends on deployment and context
2. One sensory cue cannot be evaluated in isolation
3. Design must account for proximity, intensity, and activity variations
4. Individual differences in residual vision and processing styles require multiple cue options

## Key claim from research

> "Loud noise, if close to the individual with visual impairment, is problematic because it is likely to mask desired useful sensory (audible) information. However, if the loud noise is far away, it can serve as a directional sound for orientation." [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

This exemplifies how the same stimulus (loud noise) must be understood contextually rather than categorically.

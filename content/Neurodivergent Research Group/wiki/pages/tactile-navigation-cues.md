# Tactile Navigation Cues

## Summary

Haptic sensations from ground surfaces and textures that guide people with visual impairment during navigation. Tactile cues provide directional information, warn of hazards, and serve as landmarks through texture differences encountered by cane or foot.

## Key cues

### Textured Ground Surfaces
- Rough texture to driveway or sidewalk path acting as landmarks to and from particular places
- Distinct tactile feedback from cane tapping on different floor materials
- Creates recognizable wayfinding reference points [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Truncated Domes
- Tactile warning indicators used at street edges
- Provide warning of upcoming dangers (e.g., edges of railway platforms, drop-offs)
- Alert travelers to changes in traffic direction (e.g., when crossing roads) [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

### Floor Surface Materials
- Floor surfaces providing distinct tactile and auditory feedback for cane tapping critical for accurate traveling
- Contrasting textures defining different areas within buildings
- Unique textures serving as landmarks [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

## Multi-sensory integration

Tactile cues are most effective when:
- Combined with auditory feedback (cane tapping produces sound)
- Distinct and recognizable texture changes at decision points
- Consistent placement and spacing for reliable navigation
- Used in conjunction with other sensory modalities [[context-dependent-sensory-effectiveness]]

## Design implications

Architects and designers should consider:
- Surface material choices for both tactile and auditory properties
- Strategic texture contrasts at important wayfinding locations
- Integration with tactile ground surface indicators
- Placement of suitable street furniture at appropriate intervals [[jenkins-et-al-2015-multisensory-visual-impairment]]

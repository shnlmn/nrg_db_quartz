# Co-Design in Research

## Summary

Co-design in research is a participatory method where community members (in this context, neurodivergent people) are involved as co-designers and co-creators of research materials, protocols, and methodologies—not just as subjects or post-hoc reviewers. It involves iterative cycles of feedback, implementation, and refinement to shape all aspects of research design. A [[vargas-et-al-2025-co-design-systematic-review|systematic review of 55 public health studies]] synthesizes co-design as "a collaborative and inclusive design approach that actively involves diverse stakeholders in the design process to address a specific problem, ensuring that varying degrees of participation throughout different stages lead to innovative and relevant solutions that meet the needs and expectations of all participants."

## Key claims

- Co-design originated in the 1970s from user-driven design in the USA and participatory design in Northern Europe, shifting from supplier-centred to user-centred approaches; it has since influenced architecture, education, health services, social policy, and technology [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- [[scandinavian-participatory-design|Scandinavian participatory design]] is a foundational tradition for co-design, distinguished by commitments to democracy, explicit [[values-in-design|values in design]], and treating [[conflict-as-resource-in-design|conflict as a resource]]; [[mutual-learning]] between designers and users is a core principle [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- A systematic review of 55 public health co-design studies (2016–2024) found that 44% did not define co-design, with 24 distinct definitions across those that did, highlighting significant conceptual inconsistency that hinders theoretical development and cross-study comparison [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Co-design processes typically follow 4–5 stages (51% of studies), with the basic archetype being: needs assessment (input), collaborative design (process), and prototype development (output); the Double Diamond model was the most frequently cited framework [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Applying the [[mate-taxonomy|MATE taxonomy]], most participant engagement in co-design studies was low to low-medium, with participants primarily serving as informants and consultants rather than co-designers or testers; stakeholder participation is often extractive rather than generative [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Only 7% of studies reported strategies to mitigate power dynamics (e.g. Indigenous research guidelines, separate sessions for different stakeholder groups), raising concerns about authenticity and equity of collaboration [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- To achieve generative rather than extractive co-design, research methods should increase stakeholder autonomy, contribution, and empowerment; creative and participatory methods from grey literature could improve academic practice [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Co-design with neurodivergent children as design participants reveals design insights and preferences absent from adult-only processes, demonstrating children as competent contributors and experts of their own experience [[kelly-et-al-2025-inclusive-playgrounds|Kelly et al.]]
- Genuine participation requires sharing decision-making power with co-designers; without this, participation becomes instrumental and exploitative (Keinonen 2010; Shapiro 2010); structural arrangements (resource allocation, method choice) also affect power to decide (Bratteteig & Wagner 2014) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD's [[use-before-use]] concept (Ehn 2008) means co-designers must anticipate and explore future use of artefacts that do not yet exist, through methods for telling, making, and enacting use scenarios [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Values emerge dynamically during co-design and can change, evolve, or conflict as the process unfolds; a dialogical process enables participants to question and renegotiate values rather than taking fixed positions (Iversen et al. 2012) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Attention to silences is needed in co-design processes: participants may express disagreement or discomfort through withdrawal rather than speech; sensitivity to silences is a virtue necessary for democratic design spaces (Karasti 2001; Mortberg & Stuedahl 2005) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]

- Co-design processes for participatory neurodiversity research can follow a three-phase structure: 1) partnership formation (recruiting members and defining mutual expectations); 2) iterative development (sharing documents, collecting and implementing feedback); 3) outcome reporting (creating transparency reports showing what feedback was acted upon and why) [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- To facilitate open and honest contributions in institutional settings, co-design requires explicit creation of psychological safety through mechanisms such as anonymity between members (when preferred) and mixed formal/informal communication channels allowing ad hoc contact with the research team [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Providing explanatory video walkthroughs of co-design documents can reduce cognitive overwhelm and improve accessibility for Community Advisory Board members [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- A systematic overview of 51 reviews found that co-design evaluation is rarely rigorous: 84% of reviews did not report measurement methods, and where methods existed they were mainly qualitative or informal; the [[co-design-evaluation-framework|Co-Design Evaluation Framework]] (tree metaphor) was developed to address this gap with 15 evaluation themes in 7 clusters [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- An umbrella review of 21 reviews (2015–2023) on [[digital-health-interventions|digital health]] co-design found pervasive inconsistency: terminology varied widely (co-design, participatory design, human-centered development, etc.), fewer than half of reviews reported session frequency or duration, and demographic profiles of co-design participants were scarcely reported [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Power imbalances between researchers and participants were the most cited barrier to co-design conduct in digital health; additional barriers included time/financial constraints, difficulty recruiting vulnerable populations, participant "groupthink," and privileging of medical professionals' views [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Design work historically engages "super-users" (frequent research contributors) who are often not representative of the target population, potentially exacerbating digital divides for older adults, young children, and those with low health or digital literacy [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Mitigating strategies for co-design barriers include building relationships and trust, empowering end-users, building end-user knowledge, establishing value and interest, and conducting sessions in environments familiar to participants [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- Co-design carries a risk of becoming tokenistic, especially when extra time and funding requirements are not adequately addressed [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- A rapid overview of 23 reviews found that research co-design has rarely been tested empirically or experimentally; no comparative studies measured end-user health outcomes or cost-effectiveness of co-design vs. traditional research processes [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Co-design terminology is inconsistent across the literature — [[patient-and-public-involvement]] (UK), research engagement (US), consumer involvement, stakeholder engagement, and participatory research all describe overlapping but distinct approaches, making retrieval and comparison very difficult [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Qualitative evaluations of co-design are generally positive: research topics and materials were perceived as more applicable and acceptable, end-users experienced positive emotions (confidence, pride, accomplishment), and researchers benefited from community links, improved recruitment from seldom-heard groups, and better informed consent [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Negative aspects included increased time and cost, researcher–end-user tensions, sacrificing scientific rigor for end-user preferences, and some end-users feeling "not listened to, frustrated and marginalised" [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Three barriers to co-design evaluation: (1) metrics differ from traditional health outcomes; (2) no consistency across co-design metrics; (3) no accepted theory of change linking co-design processes to terminal health/social outcomes [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Context is critical: "Different methods had varying degrees of success in a range of contexts" — effectiveness depends on attitudes, skills, and relationships between consumers and researchers, not on the co-design approach alone [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Co-design requires increased planning and longer timelines compared to traditional research design approaches, and involves higher levels of communication to ensure all voices are heard and conflicts are resolved equitably [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Although co-design creates additional constraints, it produces positive outcomes including more accessible research materials, reduced time to ethical approval (possibly due to higher quality submissions), and likely improved recruitment rates [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]
- Co-design feedback improved research accessibility in multiple dimensions: formatting (font consistency, online conversion for adjustable spacing); clarity (definitions of key terms, expanded inclusion/exclusion criteria, elaborated risks); tone (pronouns for team members, encouraging language); and structure ([[reasonable-adjustments]] section on project website) [[raw/Co-Design for Participatory Neurodiversity Research_ Collaborating With a Community Advisory Board to Design a Research Study _ Published in Journal of Participatory Resea.md|Le Cunff et al.]]

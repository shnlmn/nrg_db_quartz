# Sensory Sensitivity Exacerbating Factors

## Summary

Exacerbating factors are conditions or states that amplify or intensify [[subjective-sensory-sensitivities]]. Research identifies both situational (environmental, external) and internal (physiological, psychological, emotional) factors that increase sensitivity severity and reduce coping capacity. These factors were not explicitly prompted in the study survey but were spontaneously described by participants, suggesting high salience. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]

## Situational (Environmental) Factors

- **Unpredictability and lack of control** — uncertainty about when or what sensory stimuli will occur increases distress; the distinction between home (predictable) and public spaces (unpredictable) is particularly important. Sudden onset of stimuli and inability to control the source are key triggers. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **Focused tasks** — engaging in tasks requiring concentration (studying, assignments, reading) heightens distractibility and irritation from sensory input, particularly auditory. This is especially relevant to the impact of sensitivities on work and study. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **People and crowded environments** — being around many people or in crowded spaces (supermarkets, shops, public transport) amplifies sensory distress; crowds compound multiple sensory modalities simultaneously. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **Cumulative sensory load** — multiple simultaneous sensory inputs are more overwhelming than single modalities in isolation

## Internal Factors

- **Stress** — stress increases sensitivity, which increases stress, which further enhances sensitivity — a "sensory avalanche" cycle (Smith & Sharp 2013). Cortisol levels have been associated with sensory experience, with specific increases during novel peer interaction. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **Tiredness and fatigue** — fatigue both exacerbates sensory sensitivities and is a consequence of managing sensory overload, creating bidirectional feedback. The same bidirectional relationship has been described in acquired brain injury populations. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **Symptom change** — changes in clinical symptoms (anxiety, depression, OCD, migraine) are associated with changes in sensory sensitivity. Both causal directions are reported: symptom worsening increases sensitivity, and sensory overload worsens symptoms. [[price-et-al-2025-sensory-sensitivities]]
- **Physical health** — illness and pain increase sensory sensitivity

> **Note:** Several factors showed bidirectional causal relationships, meaning they both exacerbate sensitivities AND result from sensory overload — e.g., exhaustion triggers heightened sensitivities, which cause further exhaustion. Previous work has often emphasized only one causal direction (sensitivities causing anxiety), but participants reported both directions.

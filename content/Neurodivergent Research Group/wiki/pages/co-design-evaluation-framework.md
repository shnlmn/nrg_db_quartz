# Co-Design Evaluation Framework

## Summary

A framework developed by [[peters-et-al-2024-co-design-evaluation|Peters et al. (2024)]] using a tree metaphor to guide evaluation of research co-design processes and impacts. The below-ground structure (roots) represents the co-design group's internal processes and people, while the above-ground structure (branches/leaves) represents system- and people-level outcomes beyond the co-design group. The framework organizes 15 evaluation themes into 7 clusters and can be applied prospectively, concurrently, or retrospectively at any research phase.

## Key claims

- The tree metaphor captures the relationship between foundational co-design processes (roots) and broader outcomes (canopy): roots = People (within group), Group processes, Research processes, and Co-design context; canopy = People (outside group), System, and Sustainment [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Below-ground evaluation themes include: structure/composition of co-design group, contextual enablers/barriers, interrelationships between members, decision-making process, emotional factors, cognitive factors, value proposition, level/quality of engagement, research process, health outcomes for co-design group members, and sustainment of the team or activities [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Above-ground evaluation themes include: healthcare professional-level outcomes, healthcare system-level outcomes, organisational-level outcomes, and patient/community outcomes [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Bi-directional arrows in the trunk represent mutual influence between below-ground processes and above-ground outcomes, supporting iterative evaluation [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Which components to evaluate depends on the co-design approach and evaluation questions: early-phase co-design (e.g. priority setting) focuses on below-ground; outcome-focused studies (e.g. RCTs comparing co-designed vs. researcher-designed interventions) may focus on above-ground [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Research phases (agenda/priority setting, study design, data collection, data analysis, dissemination) run alongside the framework, indicating co-design and its evaluation can occur at any phase [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- The framework complements the [[gripp2|GRIPP2]] reporting checklist rather than replacing it [[raw/Evaluation of research co-design in health_ a systematic overview of reviews and development of a framework.md|Peters et al.]]
- Quantitative evaluation of co-design effectiveness remains challenging; the only meta-analysis identified in an umbrella review of 21 digital health co-design reviews did not support the notion that participatory-designed digital games improve health outcomes more than non-co-designed ones [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]
- In digital health co-design, participant satisfaction was infrequently assessed; when evaluated, high satisfaction was reported, but most reviews emphasized this was an infrequently measured quality metric [[raw/An umbrella review on how digital health intervention co-design is conducted and described - npj Digital Medicine.md|Kilfoy et al.]]

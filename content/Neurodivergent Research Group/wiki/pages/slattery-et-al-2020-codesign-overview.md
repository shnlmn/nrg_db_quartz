# Slattery Et Al 2020 Codesign Overview

## Summary

Slattery, Saeri & Bragge (2020) conducted a rapid overview of reviews examining research co-design in health settings, synthesizing 26 records (23 reviews) to address three questions: what co-design approaches exist, what activities they involve, and what is known about their effectiveness. Published in *Health Research Policy and Systems*, the review found co-design widely used but seldom described consistently or evaluated rigorously, and proposed the INVVOLVE mnemonic summarizing practitioner recommendations.

## Key claims

- 85% of health research funding (~$170 billion annually) is avoidably wasted through non-publication, incomplete reporting, poor design, and misalignment with end-user needs; co-design during the study planning phase is critical for averting this [[research-waste]] [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Co-design is defined as "the meaningful involvement of research users during the study planning phase," where meaningful involvement refers to "participation in an explicitly described, defined and auditable role or task necessary to the planning and/or conduct of health research" [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- The 23 included reviews used widely varying terminology — [[patient-and-public-involvement|patient and public involvement]] (UK), research engagement (US), consumer involvement, stakeholder engagement, participatory research — with significant conceptual overlap but important differences in scope, target populations, and intensity [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- No single consistent conceptualisation of "co-design" was found; Drahota et al. and Camden et al. both noted that the lack of standardized terminology makes literature retrieval and cross-study comparison very difficult [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- The most frequently reported co-design activities were [[focus-groups]], interviews, surveys, and deliberative methods (e.g. rating processes); less common activities included citizens' juries, town meetings, the nominal group technique, and the Delphi technique [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- The effectiveness of research co-design has rarely been tested empirically or experimentally; no comparative studies were found that measured end-user health outcomes or cost-effectiveness of co-design vs. traditional research processes [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Qualitative evaluations identified benefits including more applicable and acceptable research topics, improved materials, positive emotional outcomes for end-users (confidence, pride, accomplishment), and researcher benefits (community links, improved recruitment from seldom-heard groups, better informed consent) [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Negative aspects included increased time and financial resources, researcher–end-user tensions in decision-making, sacrificing scientific rigor for end-user preferences, and experiences of tokenism where end-users felt "not listened to, frustrated and marginalised" [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Co-design engagement ranged from seeking occasional, low-intensity end-user feedback to daily, intensely collaborative power-sharing arrangements in end-user-led research [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Tailoring of co-design activities to specific populations (disabled individuals, children, older patients) was recommended, but tailoring guidance for practitioner groups (doctors, nurses, carers) was virtually absent [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Three key barriers to better co-design evaluation: (1) metrics differ from traditional health outcomes; (2) lack of consistency across co-design metrics; (3) absence of an accepted causal framework or theory of change linking co-design processes to health and social outcomes [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- The INVVOLVE mnemonic synthesizes practitioner recommendations: Identify end-users, Negotiate roles and expectations, educate and train Via training, facilitate with Variety of methods, Observe and evaluate, manage Logistics and resourcing, establish communication Venues, and maintain Equitable relationships [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- The [[gripp2|GRIPP]] reporting checklist was cited as an emerging standard for reporting patient and public involvement activities [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]
- Context is critical: "Different methods had varying degrees of success in a range of contexts" — no one approach is a panacea, and effectiveness depends on attitudes, skills, and relationships between consumers and researchers [[raw/Research co-design in health_ a rapid overview of reviews - Health Research Policy and Systems.md|Slattery et al.]]

## Open questions

- What theory of change best links proximal co-design outcomes (improved materials, participant empowerment) to terminal health and social impacts?
- How should co-design activities be tailored for practitioner groups (doctors, nurses, carers) vs. patient groups?
- What is the ideal group size for different co-design activities in different contexts?

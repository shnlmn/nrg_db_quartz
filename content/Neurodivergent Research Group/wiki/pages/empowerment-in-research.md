# Empowerment in Research

## Summary

In participatory action research, empowerment is a shifting or dynamic quality of power relations between two or more people such that the relationship tends towards equity by reducing inequalities and power differences in access to resources. PAR aims to achieve empowerment by challenging established systems of surveillance and knowledge control.

## Key claims

- Labonte conceptualises empowerment as a dynamic quality of power relations tending towards equity by reducing inequalities and power differences in access to resources [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- Foucault's view is particularly relevant: power results from interactions between people, institutional practices, and exercise of different forms of knowledge [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- Foucault's work on discipline and control shows that disciplinary power functions through surveillance and internal discipline to achieve subjugation and "docility"; [[participatory-action-research|PAR]] challenges this system of surveillance and knowledge control [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- When communities seek control of research agendas and seek to be active in research, they establish themselves as more powerful agents [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- "Empowerment evaluation" argues that evaluation of health promotion should include those whose health is being promoted; while its distinctiveness has been debated, it strives to be more democratic and less expert-driven [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- Participation has been central to improving health since the WHO Health for All Strategy and was reinforced by subsequent health promotion statements [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- Power conflicts in research require "a painful self-reflective process" to handle differences in perceptions between researchers and community members, community politics, and competing interpretations of findings (Wallerstein) [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- If health services and public health organisations are to be true to principles of participation, they must initiate organisational change to improve capacity for partnership with communities [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- A [[vargas-et-al-2025-co-design-systematic-review|systematic review of 55 co-design studies]] found that only 7% reported strategies to mitigate power dynamics; most participant engagement was low to low-medium on the [[mate-taxonomy|MATE taxonomy]], suggesting co-design practice often falls short of empowerment ideals [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Co-design stakeholder participation is often extractive (gathering information without fostering ownership) rather than generative; increasing stakeholder autonomy, contribution, and empowerment through creative and participatory methods is recommended [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Decentralizing control is a key challenge in PAR: redistributing powers is both a goal and a source of tension, requiring researchers to learn to trust the process [[raw/Participatory action research - Nature Reviews Methods Primers.md|Cornish et al.]]
- Power relations — the relationships of domination, subordination, and resistance between individuals or social groups — allow some to advance their perspectives and interests more than others; PAR seeks to transform these relations [[raw/Participatory action research - Nature Reviews Methods Primers.md|Cornish et al.]]
- The [[participation-continuum]] (IAP2 Spectrum) defines "empower" as the highest level of stakeholder engagement, where decisions made by stakeholders are implemented into practice; the potential for immediate and sustainable impact rises with increasing participation toward this level [[raw/Participatory Research Methods – Choice Points in the Research Process _ Published in Journal of Participatory Research Methods.md|Vaughn & Jacquez]]
- Claims of "empowerment" through [[photovoice]] require interrogation: Wang & Burris (1994) define empowerment as requiring "at least four kinds of access: access to knowledge, access to decisions, access to networks and access to resources" — simply giving participants cameras is insufficient; framing photovoice as salvific rather than facilitative risks a deficit orientation [[raw/A systematic review of photovoice research methods with people with intellectual disabilities.md|Chinn & Balota]]
- People with [[intellectual-disability|intellectual disabilities]] face structural barriers to empowerment including socialisation into dependence, low expectations of self-determination, and exclusion from analytic processes even in nominally participatory methods [[raw/A systematic review of photovoice research methods with people with intellectual disabilities.md|Chinn & Balota]]
- Bratteteig & Wagner (2014) identify power-sharing as the most difficult part of PD: "in order to collaborate with users as co-designers, the designers need to share their power with them and acknowledge their different and equally valuable expertise"; not only people but also structural arrangements (resource allocation, method choice) affect power to decide [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- With the spread of PD to other areas, participation sometimes became an instrumental value no longer based on sharing decision-making power, becoming exploitative (Keinonen 2010; Shapiro 2010); genuine participation requires mindfulness about the issue and workings of power [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Reflexive and critical approaches to power become especially important in design projects with vulnerable groups such as children, patients, and elderly; several ethical heuristics for PD projects have been developed for iterative application (Lahti et al. 2012; Phelan & Kinsella 2013; Read et al. 2013) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]

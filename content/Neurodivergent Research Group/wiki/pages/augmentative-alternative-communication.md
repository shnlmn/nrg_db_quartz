# Augmentative Alternative Communication

## Summary

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) is any means of communication other than an established spoken or signed language, used to supplement or replace speech for people whose daily communication needs are not otherwise met. Roughly 30% of autistic children do not use verbal speech as their primary form of communication by school age, and AAC can be introduced at any age with no prerequisite skills. There is no evidence AAC slows speech development; growing evidence instead shows it supports development of speech, social, and academic skills. Systems range from no-tech (gestures, eye gaze) through paper-based/PECS and mid-tech buttons to high-tech dedicated devices or communication apps, and individuals frequently combine several types across their day.

## Key claims

- Approximately 30% of autistic children do not use verbal speech as their primary form of communication by the time they reach school age (Tager-Flusberg & Kasari, 2013). [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]
- There is no evidence that AAC use slows speech development; growing evidence shows AAC facilitates development of effective speech, social, and academic skills. [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]
- AAC helps people of all ages and has no prerequisite skills; it can be used effectively with nonspeaking autistic students from early childhood through high school (Franzone, 2008). [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]
- There is no single "best" AAC intervention for all autistic children — the key is matching a student's individual strengths and needs to appropriate type(s), often in combination. [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]
- A student with an AAC device has the same right to constant, all-environment access to communication as a student who communicates through speech. [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]
- Direct therapy provider support alone (a few sessions per week) will not make an AAC user successful in the classroom; everyday communication partners must model the system throughout the day — see [[aided-language-stimulation]]. [[raw/AAC_and_Autism_Tip_Sheet_Edu.extracted.md]]

## AAC system vs. AAC device

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **AAC system** | Encompasses all methods of communication a person uses |
| **AAC device** | The specific tool assisting communication (e.g., voice output device, pictures, communication book) |

> AAC is a tool in the same way a pencil is a tool — it does not automatically grant the ability to communicate, but can facilitate it effectively and efficiently.

## Types of AAC systems

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| No-tech | Head nods, gestures, eye gaze — nothing beyond the user's body |
| Rapid Access / Paper-Based | No electricity needed; pictures, symbols, letters, words, or phrases on paper, a single-page board, or a printed book — see [[communication-boards]] |
| [[picture-exchange-communication-system]] (PECS) | Specific protocol exchanging pictures to communicate a message; often fringe-vocabulary focused, does not build into a robust language system |
| Mid-tech | Battery-powered; produces a message on button press (e.g., single-message button, Go Talk, Quick Talker) |
| High-tech | Electronic devices with storage/retrieval of prestored messages for robust communication (dedicated devices or iPads with a communication app) |

# Inclusive Playground Design

## Summary

Approach to school and recreational playground design that deliberately accommodates diverse sensory, cognitive, social, and physical needs of all children, particularly neurodivergent, disabled, and neurodiverse users. Moves beyond physical accessibility compliance to create [[affordances|affordances]] supporting engagement, self-regulation, social connection, and wellbeing across neurotypes.

## Key Principles

From [[kelly-et-al-2025-inclusive-playgrounds|Kelly et al. 2025]], four design principles emerged through co-design with neurodivergent children:

1. **Sensory Experiences** — Features providing vestibular (spinning, swinging) and proprioceptive (climbing, pressure) feedback essential for self-regulation and engagement

2. **Challenging Opportunities** — Risky, elevated, and complex elements supporting skill development, confidence, and mastery—particularly important when risk-averse policies eliminate these

3. **Social Interaction** — Diverse participation modes from direct cooperation to parallel play and solo engagement; [[prospect-refuge|observation vantage points]] supporting anxious or socially uncertain children

4. **Natural Elements** — [[biophilic-design|Biophilic]] features including greenery, water, natural materials, and nature-based play supporting attention restoration and emotional wellbeing

## Design Features

**Essential sensory affordances**:
- Spinning and swinging structures (vestibular regulation)
- Elevated climbing and seating (proprioceptive feedback, prospect-refuge)
- Varied natural materials and textures (tactile engagement)
- Quiet withdrawal spaces or sensory refuges (recovery from overstimulation)
- Open sightlines and seating for social flexibility (parallel play options)

**Environmental considerations**:
- Pathways and accessible design accommodating varied mobility and navigation needs
- Acoustic management reducing anthropogenic noise
- Natural lighting preferred over artificial fluorescence
- Varied spatial density (open areas and enclosed zones)
- Imaginative and nature-based elements alongside traditional equipment

## Barriers in Current Practice

- **Safety-averse institutional policies** — Removal of challenging elements (flying foxes, sand pits, height) eliminates essential [[affordances|affordances]] for vestibular and proprioceptive development, despite children's capacity to assess and mitigate risk
- **Neurotypical design defaults** — Standards designed for average sensory tolerance exclude children with hypersensitivities or those requiring higher sensory input
- **Sensory overwhelm** — Inadequate withdrawal spaces, excessive noise, unregulated lighting create barriers for sensory-sensitive children
- **Social inflexibility** — Designs assuming direct peer interaction exclude socially anxious or autistic children preferring observation or solo play
- **Post-pandemic gaps** — Insufficient attention to social isolation recovery and rising mental health needs in young people aged 10–14

## Benefits of Implementation

- **Neurodivergent children experience longer engagement** and positive social outcomes when environments support their sensory and regulatory needs
- **All children benefit** from diverse sensory affordances, flexible participation modes, and [[biophilic-design|nature-based elements]]
- **School communities benefit** from reduced behavioral disruption, improved inclusivity, and strengthened overall wellbeing
- **Equitable research methods** reveal design insights absent from adult-only planning; co-design with children produces more responsive solutions

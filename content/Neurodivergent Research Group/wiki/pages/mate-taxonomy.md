# Mate Taxonomy

## Summary

The MATE (Methodology, Agent of change, Training, and Engagement) taxonomy is a framework for understanding and classifying participatory design applications according to the methods used and their potential to facilitate meaningful participant engagement. Developed by Willmott, it categorizes participant roles and engagement levels rather than evaluating outcomes.

## Key claims

- The MATE taxonomy defines six participant roles (agents of change): informant (provides specialized knowledge), consultant (helps frame and solve problems), co-designer (engaged at various stages to provide insight and feedback), tester (provides feedback on prototypes), leader (reflexively participates in select stages), and trainee (receives training before actively collaborating) [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Engagement is classified on a low–medium–high continuum based on three factors: agency, input, and empowerment [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Low engagement methods include surveys, ethnography, and interviews; medium engagement includes focus groups, stakeholder consultation, co-creation/co-design workshops, and usability testing; high engagement includes peer leadership, [[participatory-action-research]], community-based participatory research, and citizen science [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- When applied to 55 public health co-design studies, the taxonomy revealed that most participant engagement was low to low-medium, with participants primarily serving as informants and consultants rather than co-designers or testers [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- The taxonomy is not intended to evaluate participatory design outcomes; instead it classifies applications according to methods more likely to facilitate meaningful engagement [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]

# Extractive Research

## Summary

Research that extracts information and exploits relationships, places, and peoples, producing benefit for scholars or institutions elsewhere while depleting resources at the sites of the research. Extractive research is a central concern in [[participatory-action-research|PAR]] and [[decolonization-in-research|decolonization]] discourse.

## Key claims

- Extractive research produces benefit for scholars or institutions elsewhere and depletes resources at the sites of the research [[raw/Participatory action research - Nature Reviews Methods Primers.md|Cornish et al.]]
- PAR explicitly counters extractive approaches by ensuring community participation, leadership, and benefit from the research process [[raw/Participatory action research - Nature Reviews Methods Primers.md|Cornish et al.]]
- A [[vargas-et-al-2025-co-design-systematic-review|systematic review of co-design studies]] found that stakeholder participation is often extractive (gathering information without fostering ownership) rather than generative [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- In Indigenous health research, PAR has potential to reduce colonising effects of conventional research by avoiding exploitation, ensuring community control, specifying benefits, and preventing misrepresentation [[raw/Participatory action research.md|Baum et al.]]
- Majority-group researchers studying marginalized populations have historically produced biased scholarship; neurotypical researchers are likely to publish information that further marginalizes neurodivergent people because they lack the lived experience of being neurodivergent themselves — a form of epistemic extraction that frames neurodivergent people as objects of study while centering neurotypical interpretive frameworks [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Ableist language in clinical neurodiversity research (comparisons with "great apes," "healthy/normal" controls, "high/low functioning" labels) reflects systemic and unconscious bias that can hinder the capacity of professionals to serve the populations their work intends to benefit [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]

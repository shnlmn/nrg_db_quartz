# CBPR Applied to Neurodivergent Research

## Summary

Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR) applied to neurodivergent communities requires treating neurodivergent lived experience as a primary knowledge source, designing environments that actively enable epistemic participation, and sharing decision-making authority across all research phases. It is an ethical requirement given the history of extractive, deficit-focused, neurotypical-normed research the community has experienced.

---

## What CBPR Is

CBPR equitably involves community members, researchers, and stakeholders across **all phases** of research — design, data collection, analysis, and dissemination. It is not consultation. It is not handing a survey to a focus group. It aspires to the "collaborate" or "empower" end of the [[participation-continuum]], where communities share or hold decision-making authority.

CBPR has two foundational traditions:
- **Northern:** Kurt Lewin's [[action-research]] — cyclical reflection and action within communities [[collins-et-al-2018-cbpr-psychology]]
- **Southern:** Freire/Fals Borda liberation pedagogy — challenging who controls knowledge, and for whose benefit [[collins-et-al-2018-cbpr-psychology]]

These traditions converge on a core conviction: research *done to* marginalized communities perpetuates the same power structures those communities experience everywhere else.

---

## Why It Matters for Neurodivergent Communities

Neurodivergent people have historically been **objects** of research, not participants in it. The [[medical-model-of-disability]] framing — deficit-focused, diagnostic, neurotypical-normed — has dominated. CBPR directly challenges this through three interlocking principles:

### 1. Epistemic Justice

[[epistemic-justice]] holds that neurodivergent people are credible experts on their own experience, processing styles, and communication preferences. Epistemic injustice occurs when medicalized views or neurotypical communication norms silence or distort that expertise. CBPR operationalizes epistemic justice by treating neurodivergent lived experience as a primary knowledge source, not a variable to be measured by outsiders.

### 2. Epistemic Enablement

Knowing that epistemic justice matters is not enough. Researchers must design environments that actively enable neurodivergent epistemic participation ([[epistemic-enablement]]). This means:
- Adapting to participants' processing needs rather than expecting adaptation to neurotypical norms
- Accommodating [[communication-preferences]], sensory needs, and emotional safety requirements
- Treating these not as accommodations but as foundational design conditions

### 3. Relational Inclusion

[[relational-inclusion]] holds that inclusion cannot be achieved through policies, checklists, or accessibility statements alone. It is enacted through relationships and trust. For neurodivergent research partners this means:
- Consistent presence and follow-through ("showing up" on the community's terms [[community-based-participatory-research]])
- Environments that feel safe sensorially and interpersonally
- Ongoing negotiation of power throughout the research lifecycle, not a one-time consent process

## The Seven CBPR Principles Applied to Neurodivergent Research

From [[collins-et-al-2018-cbpr-psychology]]:

| Principle | Neurodivergent Application |
|---|---|
| **Community as unit of identity** | Neurodivergent identity is a valid community identity, not just a diagnostic category |
| **Cultural humility** | Addresses neurotypical norms, ableism, and the [[medical-model-of-disability]] gaze alongside race/class/ethnicity |
| **Ecological perspective** | Recognizes that environment, sensory factors, and social context shape neurodivergent experience |
| **Equitable partnership** | Neurodivergent people co-own research questions, methods, and outputs |
| **Community strengths** | Rejects deficit framing; centers divergent cognition as variation, not pathology |
| **Cyclical/iterative process** | Methods adjusted as community partners identify barriers to participation |
| **Sustainable change** | Research generates actionable benefit for neurodivergent people, not just academic outputs |

## Practical Implementation

### Build Trust Before Research

CBPR begins before any study question is formed. For neurodivergent communities:
- Consistent presence over time, beyond formal meetings [[community-based-participatory-research]]
- Demonstrating researcher agendas are genuinely subordinate to community priorities
- Recognizing many neurodivergent people have been burned by extractive research or token consultation ([[co-option-in-research]])

### Community Advisory Boards as Structure

[[community-advisory-board]]s operationalize CBPR. In neurodivergent research (Le Cunff et al.'s co-design work, cited in [[psychological-safety-in-research]]):
- CAB members identified accessibility issues (e.g., formatting problems for dyslexic members) the research team had missed
- [[psychological-safety-in-research|Psychological safety]] mechanisms must be explicit, not assumed
- Mixed formal/informal communication channels are more effective than meeting-only structures
- Anonymity between members can reduce interpersonal risk but may impede shared ownership — tension requiring active management

### Communication and Method Accessibility

Standard research methods exclude neurodivergent people by design ([[inclusive-research-methods]]):
- Surveys demanding strong recall are problematic for many ADHD presentations (see [[recall-bias]])
- Focus groups misread behavioral cues (eye contact aversion, stimming) as disengagement or conflict
- Narrow response formats impose neurotypical expressiveness norms

CBPR with neurodivergent partners requires:
- Asking "How do you prefer to be communicated with?" at the outset
- Offering multiple modalities (written, verbal, visual, asynchronous, real-time)
- Accounting for [[alexithymia]] — offering art, physical sensation descriptions, or other non-linguistic expression channels when emotion articulation is the goal
- Specific, unambiguous language to address the [[double-empathy-problem]] — vague questions create information overload, not richer data
- [[reasonable-adjustments]] made standard, not exceptional: breaks, multiple participation modes, written ground rules alongside verbal ones

### Capacity Building

CBPR must develop community skills and resources that outlast the project ([[capacity-building]]):
- Training neurodivergent co-researchers in research methods
- Ensuring neurodivergent partners build transferable skills (analysis, dissemination, advocacy)
- Creating sustainable structures (e.g., ongoing CABs) rather than project-duration advisory roles

### Reflexivity and Power

Researchers must continuously examine their own positionality — including their neurotypicality ([[reflexivity]]). The [[double-empathy-problem]] means miscommunication is bidirectional; researchers should not default to assuming their interpretation of a participant's behavior or data is accurate. [[critical-reflection]] and team-level reflexivity practices help surface where neurotypical assumptions are shaping research design.

## Key Risks to Guard Against

**[[co-option-in-research]]:** The greatest failure mode. Adopting participatory language while directing the process toward institutional or researcher interests. Countered by building a critical community that can hold researchers to account, practicing [[reflexivity]], and being genuinely willing to change the research question when community partners say it is wrong.

**[[research-waste]]:** Neurodivergent communities have often experienced research that produces no benefit to them. Biomedical interventions average 17 years from research to practice; CBPR closes this gap by creating interventions *with* the community rather than *for* it [[community-based-participatory-research]].

**Validity myths:** CBPR does not compromise internal validity. A review of 60 community health studies confirmed this — CBPR often improves validity by enhancing recruitment and retention in marginalized populations (Viswanathan et al. 2004) [[community-based-participatory-research]].

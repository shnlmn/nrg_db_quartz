# Designing Spaces for Neurodivergent People

> [!info] Promoted from a query. Verify against source pages before citing.

## Summary

A synthesis of neuro-inclusive design principles oriented toward architectural practice. Approximately 15–20% of the population is neurodivergent, and **95% of autistic individuals demonstrate atypical sensory processing** ([[sensory-processing-autism]]). Most design guidelines stop at ADA compliance, which addresses physical access but not the cognitive and sensory dimensions of inclusion ([[neuro-inclusive-design]]). The built environment is not neutral — spaces that feel unremarkable to neurotypical users can be actively hostile to neurodivergent people through noise, lighting, crowding, and unpredictable layouts ([[hypersensitivity]], [[sensory-sensitivity-exacerbating-factors]]).

## Core Principle: Design from the Sensorial Margins

Rather than designing for the "average" user and retrofitting accommodations, **design from the sensorial margins** — center the needs of people with the most extreme sensory sensitivities first ([[design-from-sensorial-margins]]). Solutions that work for people at the margins work for everyone. This is the architectural equivalent of the curb-cut effect.

## Key Claims

- **Sensory zoning** is essential: a single sensory environment cannot serve everyone. Buildings and sites should provide high-stimulation zones, low-stimulation zones / [[sensory-refuge|sensory refuges]], and transitional buffers between them ([[sensory-zoning]], [[sensory-affordances]])
- **Biophilic integration** is consistently reported as calming and restorative by neurodivergent users: natural light over fluorescent, greenery and water features, natural materials (wood, stone) over synthetic, and living systems for engagement ([[biophilic-design]]). This aligns with Attention Restoration Theory and Stress Reduction Theory
- **Predictability and wayfinding** reduce cognitive load: clear and consistent signage, logical layouts, multi-sensory cues (tactile, auditory, olfactory landmarks), unambiguous spatial organization, and consistent lighting and acoustics ([[cognitive-load]], [[executive-functioning]], [[universal-design-public-space]])
- **All eight sensory systems** must be addressed, not just visual aesthetics: visual (glare control, dimmable lighting), auditory (acoustic management, sound absorption), olfactory (ventilation, avoiding off-gassing), tactile (material variety, temperature), proprioceptive (pressure-feedback seating, varied terrain), vestibular (rocking chairs, swings, varied levels), interoceptive (temperature regulation, access to food/water/restrooms), and gustatory (food service and therapeutic settings) ([[sensory-responsive-design]])

## The SREF Framework

The [[sensory-responsive-environments-framework|Sensory Responsive Environments Framework]] (Finnigan, 2024) provides a comprehensive, non-prescriptive approach built on four pillars:

1. **Design from the Sensorial Margins** — Center extreme sensory needs
2. **Sensory Zoning** — Diversified zones with varied affordances
3. **Nature-Based Approaches** — Biophilic elements for restorative benefit
4. **Co-Design** — Include neurodivergent people in the design process as experts on their own experience

The framework is deliberately non-prescriptive — it provides principles adaptable to context, climate, culture, and building type rather than a fixed checklist.

## Salutogenic Approach

Traditional design asks "How do we prevent harm?" A [[salutogenic-design|salutogenic approach]] asks "How do we actively generate wellbeing?" This means designing spaces that support **self-regulation** (movement, pressure, calm), offering **choice and autonomy** over one's sensory environment, and creating environments where neurodivergent people can stay longer, engage more deeply, and feel they belong ([[relational-inclusion]]).

Evidence from inclusive playground co-design shows that when environments match regulatory needs, neurodivergent users stay engaged longer, form positive social connections, and report improved wellbeing ([[kelly-et-al-2025-inclusive-playgrounds]]).

## The Case for Universal Benefit

Neuro-inclusive design is not a niche accommodation — it is better design for everyone. Quieter spaces, clearer wayfinding, natural materials, and sensory choice improve the experience for all building users, including aging populations, people with temporary injuries, and anyone having a stressful day ([[universal-design-public-space]]).

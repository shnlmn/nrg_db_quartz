# Mutual Learning

## Summary

A core principle of [[scandinavian-participatory-design|Scandinavian participatory design]] and [[participatory-action-research|participatory action research]] in which designers/researchers and users/participants learn from each other through collaborative engagement. Designers learn about work practices and local knowledge; users learn about design possibilities and technological alternatives.

## Key claims

- Mutual learning between and among designers and users is a core principle in working relations in Scandinavian PD [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Creating preconditions for viable participation includes working to bring forward tacit knowledge and shared knowledge that is taken for granted and therefore usually unspoken or invisible [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Cooperative approaches argue that "workplace language and daily experience of users need to be placed centre stage in an effort to enable users. For enabling users implies not just using their experience, but creating and fostering an environment where they can feel empowered to express their ideas" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Susanne Bødker frames design as "fundamentally a collective activity, in which the various practices of the participants meet in a process of mutual learning. This meeting creates conflicts that create new possibilities in design" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- In the HISP project, working with users to understand and fix errors represented opportunities for localisation and mutual learning; not all entries that appeared to be "errors" were in fact errors [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- PAR provides significant inspiration for PD practices "particularly in emphases on mutual learning and reciprocity in relationships" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- In mutual learning, participants not only share practical knowledge, competences, and values but "also learn more about their work themselves" (Karasti 2001); PD workshops provide analytic distance allowing articulation of meanings and discovery of previously invisible taken-for-granted aspects of routine practices [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Embodied knowledge implicated in people's day-to-day work becomes visible through participatory activities (Jansson 2007) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- There is a risk of falling back into dualities that keep apart designers and users; the dominant discourse tends to focus on users and use context without paying attention to designers' own values and norms (Markussen 1996) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Suchman (2002) argues for "design from somewhere": knowledge, including professional expertise, is partial and situated (Haraway 1988), dependent on practice, history, and participants' views — countering the notion of a disembodied "design from nowhere" where the professional designer is invisible yet located everywhere [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]

# Bernard Et Al 2023 Inclusion Ethics

## Summary

A 2023 commentary in *Industrial and Organizational Psychology* (Vol. 16, Issue 1, pp. 50–54; DOI: 10.1017/iop.2022.100) arguing that neurodiversity research conducted without neurodivergent collaborators risks reproducing harmful, neurotypical-biased assumptions. The authors recommend (a) including neurodivergent research team members and (b) strengthening participant influence through qualitative and participatory action research methods when (a) is not feasible.

## Key claims

- Majority-group researchers have historically produced biased scholarship on novel marginalized populations; neurotypical researchers lack the lived experience of being neurodivergent and are likely to publish information that further marginalizes neurodivergent people [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Clinical literature has characterized autistic people as "emotionless" and grouped them with "great apes"; studies routinely compare neurodivergent individuals to "healthy" or "normal" controls, implying deviance or disorder [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Despite community disapproval (see Kenny et al. 2016), "high/low functioning" terminology persists in autism and ADHD literature; such ableist language can impact societal perception and contribute to stigmatization [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- A single listserv request to one I-O psychology professional network produced six PhD students and recent graduates with neurodivergent identities willing to collaborate — evidence that neurodivergent researchers are readily available with minimal effort [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Neurodivergent team members commonly mask identity-stereotypical behaviors, exacting a toll on their personal resources and wellbeing; neurotypical collaborators should actively reduce the need to assimilate [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Applying the [[canary-code]] to research teams means: collectively determining detailed project completion plans; focusing on task completion rather than execution style; remaining flexible on time, place, and work style; discussing task assignments, methods, and authorship in advance; sharing notes inclusively; and making compensation decisions transparently based on performance outcomes rather than implicit interpersonal politics [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Per APA guidelines, anyone involved in initial research design, data collection, manuscript drafting, or final approval makes a substantial contribution; community representatives who review manuscripts for final approval may qualify for authorship; when authorship is not desired, other compensation is owed for expertise and time [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Qualitative methods benefit neurodiversity research by enabling open responses less restricted than pre-generated quantitative scales, which can reflect neurotypical researchers' preconceived notions and may fail to capture what matters most to neurodivergent participants [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- [[participatory-action-research|Participatory action research]] (PAR) aims to break down research hierarchies by deliberately sharing power; participants can influence topic selection, data collection, analysis, and action decisions; in organizational contexts PAR can improve working environments and alter discriminatory policies [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- Neurodivergent people produce informal knowledge (blogs, online forums, consulting, manuscript reviewing) that neurotypical researchers often do not access; this expertise often goes unrecognized and should be acknowledged and compensated [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- The authors prefer "neurodivergent" over "neuroatypical" on accessibility grounds: the terms differ by only one letter, a confusion risk for dyslexic readers; one author did not initially realize the focal article was about neuroatypicality rather than neurotypicality [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]
- The paper is a commentary responding to LeFevre-Levy et al. (2023) "Neurodiversity in the workplace: Considering neuroatypicality as a form of diversity" in the same issue; author identities include autism, CPTSD, neurodivergence, sensory processing disorder, anxiety, and ADHD [[raw/Not your 'typical' research_ Inclusion ethics in neurodiversity scholarship _ Industrial and Organizational Psychology|Bernard et al. 2023]]

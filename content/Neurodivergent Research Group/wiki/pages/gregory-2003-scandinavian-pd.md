# Gregory 2003 Scandinavian Pd

## Summary

Gregory, J. (2003). "Scandinavian Approaches to Participatory Design." *International Journal of Engineering Education*, 19(1), 62–74. A review arguing that three principles distinguish Scandinavian participatory design from other user-participation traditions: deep commitments to democracy and democratisation; explicit discussions of values in design and imagined futures; and treating conflict and contradictions as resources in design. Illustrates these principles through two case studies: the Health Information Systems Project (HISP) in South Africa and the International Women's University (IFU) health information project.

## Key claims

- Scandinavian participatory design is distinguished not by particular methods but by political commitments to societal concerns and relationships with participating users and communities [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Three principles distinguish Scandinavian PD: (1) deep commitments to democracy and democratisation; (2) explicit discussions of [[values-in-design|values in design]] and imagined futures; (3) regarding [[conflict-as-resource-in-design|conflict and contradictions as resources]] in design [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- The participatory design movement in Scandinavia has roots in post-war political movements striving for industrial democracy, including forms of co-determination by unions and workers in decision-making [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Leading early projects include the Norwegian Iron and Metal Workers' Union technology project (1970), DEMOS in Sweden (1975–1979), DUE in Denmark (1977–1980), UTOPIA (1981–1984), and the Florence project (1984–1987) [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- [[paulo-freire]]'s pedagogy for critical consciousness and liberation was a contributing influence to the participatory design movement [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- [[action-research]] and [[participatory-action-research]] provide significant inspirations and broad methodological grounding for participatory design practices, particularly in emphases on [[mutual-learning]] and reciprocity [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- A shift "from the political to the ethical system developer" occurred in the second generation of Scandinavian PD projects in the 1980s, as arenas for participation shifted from structural institutions to specific workplace situations [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Bjerknes and Bratteteig (1995) warn that a democratic process does not always give a democratic result; without organisational reform towards greater democratisation at all levels, PD risks becoming just "a number of local methods for designing IT systems" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- The HISP District Health Information System (DHIS) in South Africa deliberately allowed users to make mistakes in order to promote continuous learning, local autonomy, and empowerment — contrasting sharply with US design traditions focused on eliminating "human error" through automation [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Braa and Hedberg conceptualise participatory design as cultivation: "a slow incremental bottom-up process of gradually transforming social structures and information infrastructures where the resources already available form the base" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Lucy Suchman describes the Scandinavian PD orientation: "designing computer artefacts is an inherently value-based activity, deeply implicated in longstanding political struggles of the wider society" — contrasting with the US mainstream view of design as "strictly technical and commercial arenas" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Susanne Bødker frames conflict in design as a resource for creativity: "Design is fundamentally a collective activity, in which the various practices of the participants meet in a process of mutual learning. This meeting creates conflicts that create new possibilities in design" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Scandinavian PD emphasises imagined futures and change in social practices, not only technological change; design is conceptualised in relation to continuous learning and change rather than narrowly in relation to a product or system [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Creating preconditions for viable participation includes working to bring forward tacit knowledge and shared knowledge that is taken for granted and therefore usually unspoken or invisible [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- By the late 1990s, the notion of worker-controlled resources and independent worker activities as a strategy for influence had "almost totally disappeared," posing challenges for PD's democratic aspirations [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]

# Scandinavian Participatory Design

## Summary

A tradition of participatory design originating in Scandinavian countries in the 1970s, distinguished from other user-participation approaches not by particular methods but by three principles: deep commitments to democracy and democratisation, explicit discussions of [[values-in-design|values in design]] and imagined futures, and treating [[conflict-as-resource-in-design|conflict and contradictions as resources]] in design. Rooted in post-war industrial democracy movements and trade union collaboration with computer scientists.

## Key claims

- Scandinavian PD has roots in post-war political movements striving for industrial democracy, including co-determination by unions and workers in workplace decision-making [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Key early projects: Norwegian Iron and Metal Workers' Union technology project (1970), DEMOS (Sweden, 1975–1979), DUE (Denmark, 1977–1980), UTOPIA (1981–1984), and the Florence project with nurses (1984–1987) [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- PD methods are diverse — including design-by-doing, mock-up envisionment, future workshops, organisational games, cooperative prototyping, ethnographic field research, and democratic dialogue — but the tradition is distinguished by political commitments, not particular methods [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Pelle Ehn: "In the interest of emancipation, we deliberately made the choice of siding with workers and their organisations, supporting the development of their resources for a change towards democracy at work" [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- [[mutual-learning]] between designers and users is a core principle in working relations; creating preconditions for participation includes surfacing tacit and taken-for-granted knowledge [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Scandinavian PD emphasises change and development of people, organisations, and practices — not only technological change — occurring in changing socio-historical contexts [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- A second-generation shift "from the political to the ethical system developer" occurred in the 1980s as arenas for participation moved from structural institutions to specific workplace situations; without broader organisational reform, PD risks becoming merely local design methods [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- [[paulo-freire]]'s pedagogy for critical consciousness and [[action-research]]/[[participatory-action-research]] provide significant inspiration and methodological grounding [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- By the late 1990s, worker-controlled resources and independent worker activities as a strategy for influence had "almost totally disappeared," challenging PD's democratic aspirations [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Social movements (women's, environmental, multicultural diversity, NGOs) may provide more viable bases than trade unions for alliances to realise democracy in design [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- Also known as the Collective Resource Approach, Cooperative Design, and Cooperative Experimental System Development [[raw/IJEE1353.extracted.md|Gregory 2003]]
- van der Velden & Mortberg formulate six guiding principles of PD: (1) equalising power relations; (2) democratic practices; (3) situation-based action (attending to people's embodied and situated expertise of day-to-day activities); (4) [[mutual-learning]]; (5) tools and techniques (participatory methods creating inclusive design spaces); (6) alternative visions about technology [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD is defined (Simonsen & Robertson 2012) as "a process of investigating, understanding, reflecting upon, establishing, developing, and supporting mutual learning between multiple participants in collective 'reflection-in-action'" [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- Bjerknes & Bratteteig (1995) identified a turn from politics to ethics: the system developer shifted from emancipator in a collective political process to professional facilitator; Steen (2013) calls this the "ethical turn" in PD [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- PD expanded from Scandinavian system development to urban planning, architecture, sustainable development, healthcare, and global settings; the meaning of participation has changed significantly with more knowledgeable co-designers and global networks [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- In multicultural settings, PD's principle values of participation and democracy need to be explored in local context; in hierarchical societies, participation is not necessarily associated with democracy (Winschiers-Theophilus et al. 2012) [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]
- The core theme of PD (Ehn 1988) is addressing the dialectics of tradition and transcendence: the tension between what is and what could be; PD's methods enable participants to anticipate future use and alternative futures [[raw/Participatory_Design_and_Design_for_Valu.extracted.md|van der Velden & Mortberg]]

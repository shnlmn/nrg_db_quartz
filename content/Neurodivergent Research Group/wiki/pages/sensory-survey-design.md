# Sensory Survey Design

## Summary

Systematic approach to planning and executing comprehensive documentation of sensory experiences within a research site or across multiple locations. Integrates decisions about spatial scope, temporal variation, measurement strategies (point vs. transect), researcher teams, and methodological sequencing. Develops transparent, replicable protocols for identifying and recording sensory phenomena across multiple sensory modalities.

## Key Claims

- **Survey design must reflect research questions and desired outcomes:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Objective choice: analysis (subjective-focused) vs. reproduction (objective/technical measurement)
  - Method choice: single vs. multiple sensory modalities; separate walks vs. integrated multisensory transects
  - Scale choice: point measurements, [[sensory-transect]] surveys, or combined approach

- **Spatial scale determines data collection procedures:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Confined indoor spaces (e.g., winery, chapel)
  - Open indoor spaces (e.g., market bazaar, museum)
  - Confined outdoor locations (e.g., fountain plaza, pathway)
  - Large open outdoor locations (e.g., garden, park, festival site)
  - Each type requires different sensory documentation strategy

- **Point measurements provide comparative baseline data:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Fixed positions capture significant/iconic sensory sources or events
  - Determined through site reconnaissance or prior research
  - Enable comparison across time periods (diurnal, seasonal)
  - Location selection can be adjusted based on pilot findings

- **Sensory transects capture spatial transformation:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Researcher/participant walks predetermined or emerging routes
  - Documents presence and change in sensory experiences
  - Length determined by research question feasibility
  - Multiple transects may be employed over different temporal periods

- **Pilot studies are critical for method refinement:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Test methodological decisions (individual vs. combined sensewalks; silent vs. discussion-based walks)
  - Trial documentation methods (aural, visual, descriptive)
  - Improve fixed-point positions
  - Multiple visits optimal, conducted under similar temporal dynamics as main study
  - Especially important when considerable distances/expenses or repeated data collection unfeasible

- **Research sequence (sensory identification order) impacts data quality:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Five separate sensewalks: highly focused identification, exceptional attention, but extensive time commitment and temporal lags between multisensory comparisons
  - Multisensory transects: capture integrated experience authenticity, faster turnover enabling greater site coverage, but reduced concentration on individual senses
  - Choice should be informed by physiological testing results if available

- **Temporal variation is essential variable requiring intentional documentation:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - **Diurnal factors:** lighting, ambient temperature, time-dependent activities, crowd/tourist flux, meal-specific cooking practices, socially-determined drinking practices
  - **Seasonal factors:** temperature variation, foliage color and coverage, tourist/visitor flux, seasonally-distinct foods/produce, annual patterns
  - **Intra-period variation:** weekday/weekend effects on crowd behavior, visitor excitement
  - **Timing guidance:** To explore temporal changes within one season, collect data over multiple days in repeated blocks with wide temporal scale; for exploratory overviews, collect from "hot spot" areas during peak sensory activity

- **Site selection should be purposeful and justified:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Single vs. multiple locations determined by research questions
  - Selection based on researchers' prior work, relevant literature, or specific research hypotheses
  - Locations validated through research design

- **Observer characteristics affect perception and require mitigation:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Perception involves both sensory detection (physiological: proximity, olfactory acuity) and sense-making (mental: cultural background, attention, localness)
  - Team composition affects outcome: local observers have greater cultural awareness; nonlocal observers overcome [[smell-habituation]] and bring fresh perspective
  - Optimal: dual researcher pairs combining local + nonlocal expertise
  - Physiological calibration: pre-study olfactory screening, taste tests, touch-centered sensory testing to account for individual differences
  - Solo, pair, or group documentation possible; pairs/groups recommended to minimize individual bias

- **Transparent methodological documentation enables replication:** [[raw/parker-et-al-2024-the-identification-and-documentation-of-on-site-sensory-and-multisensory-experience-a-methodological.extracted.md]]
  - Checklist protocol specifies: locational details (scaled maps, access points); site selection rationale; included sensory components and omission justification; timing/duration documentation; data collection methods; transect/point positions; equipment specifics; research sequence justification; researcher demographics; observer bias acknowledgment and mitigation

## Decision Tree

The paper presents a procedural decision summary (Figure 1) guiding researchers through:
1. Research question clarification
2. Sensory component selection
3. Spatial and temporal scope definition
4. Measurement approach (point vs. transect vs. combination)
5. Data collection sequencing (single-sense vs. multisensory)
6. Observer team composition
7. Pilot study confirmation
8. Protocol implementation

All decisions informed by prior research, team knowledge/insight, and refined through pilot phases.

# Sensory-Friendly Programming

## Summary

Sensory-friendly programming refers to planned modifications to institutional services, spaces, and schedules that reduce sensory and social barriers for autistic and otherwise sensory-sensitive users. Most documented implementations occur in libraries and museums (ALM sector). Provisions range from quiet hours and sensory kits to dedicated study rooms and staff training.

## Key claims

- Special hours with dimmed lighting, lower volume, and limited patron access are "a common and effective method for cutting out lots of stimuli" (Shrikant 2018, citing Smithsonian practice). [[raw/svaler-2023-on-making-libraries-and-museums-more-accessible-for-autistic-people.extracted.md]]
- A permanent programme schedule brings routine, which helps autistic people deal with transitions and challenges. [[raw/svaler-2023-on-making-libraries-and-museums-more-accessible-for-autistic-people.extracted.md]]
- Pre-visit videos reduce the impact of unexpected occurrences; informing users about quieter times and quiet spaces allows advance preparation. [[raw/svaler-2023-on-making-libraries-and-museums-more-accessible-for-autistic-people.extracted.md]]
- Even minimal changes (sensory activities, quiet hours) are "wonderful for making the museum experience better for children on the spectrum" (Freed-Brown 2010). [[raw/svaler-2023-on-making-libraries-and-museums-more-accessible-for-autistic-people.extracted.md]]
- Sensory kits/bags benefit all patrons while still maintaining the library as a social public sphere. [[raw/svaler-2023-on-making-libraries-and-museums-more-accessible-for-autistic-people.extracted.md]]

## Provision taxonomy

### Temporal
- **Quiet/sensory-friendly hours**: early or restricted-access sessions; dimmed lighting; lower volumes; limited patron numbers
- **Visitor density information**: statistical when-is-it-quiet data on website; quieter vs. busier time guidance

### Environmental/spatial
- **Non-fluorescent / alternative lighting**: LED strips, bubble tubes, dimmable options, natural light
- **Sensory study rooms**: varied lighting, varied seating (rocking chairs, wobble cushions, soft seating)
- **Quiet/decompression spaces**: areas to recover from sensory overload; gardens or adjacent rooms
- **Study space maps and availability maps**: shows which spaces are currently free; reduces navigational uncertainty
- **Sensory maps**: room-by-room lighting, noise, temperature, crowd-level information (e.g., British Museum)

### Equipment/kits
- **Sensory support kits/bags**: noise-cancelling headphones, fidget tools, weighted lap pads, verbal cue cards
- **Sensory backpacks** (museums): activity booklets + engagement tools per exhibit theme
- **Accessible software**: Audio Notetaker, Read&Write Gold, MindManager (e.g., University of Warwick Library)
- Future consideration: sunglasses, stim toys for teens/adults (currently underserved)

### Informational
- **Pre-visit videos**: prepare users for what to expect, rules, routines, safety
- **Accessibility web pages**: quieter times, sensory-friendly areas, booking sensory rooms
- **Clear rules overview**: helps autistic users understand how to use institutional space
- **Handouts / signage**: in-space guides to quiet areas and sensory provisions

### Social/service
- **Chat reference**: alternative to face-to-face reference desk; reduces social interaction demand
- **Personal librarians**: dedicated support contact
- **Guaranteed same study space**: supports predictability and routine
- **Special interest groups**: safe social engagement around shared interests

### Staff
- **Sensory-inclusion training**: e.g., KultureCity nonprofit programme
- **Autism awareness education**: librarians learning about ASD, tailoring instruction, outreach to autistic students
- **Collaboration with autism support programmes**: especially in academic settings

## Documented implementations

| Institution | Programme |
|---|---|
| Smithsonian | "Morning at the Museum" (2011–); pre-visit videos |
| British Museum | Sensory map; quieter-times guidance; sensory-support backpacks |
| University of Warwick Library | Sensory study rooms; accessible software; booking video |
| Multnomah County Library | Sensory kits (children); sensory-friendly hour |
| Pickerington Public Library | Sensory bags; KultureCity staff training |

## Open questions

- Do autistic adults benefit as much as children from sensory-friendly museum programmes? (most documented examples target children)
- What is the cost-effectiveness of sensory-friendly hour programmes relative to their impact?
- How can institutions gather feedback from autistic users to iteratively improve provisions?

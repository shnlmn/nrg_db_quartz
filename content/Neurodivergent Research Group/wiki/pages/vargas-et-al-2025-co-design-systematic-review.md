# Vargas Et Al 2025 Co-Design Systematic Review

## Summary

Systematic review by Vargas, Zorbas, Longworth, Ugalde, Needham, Sunil, Venegas Hargous, Bennett, Forrester-Bowling, Cardoso Richter, and Allender (*Journal of Public Health*, 2025) synthesizing 55 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2024) on the use of co-design as a process in public health. The review examines co-design definitions, processes, frameworks, and stakeholder participation using the [[mate-taxonomy|MATE taxonomy]].

## Key claims

- 44% of included studies did not define co-design; among the 56% that did, 24 distinct definitions were identified, highlighting significant conceptual inconsistency [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- The authors synthesize a definition: co-design is "a collaborative and inclusive design approach that actively involves diverse stakeholders in the design process to address a specific problem, ensuring that varying degrees of participation throughout different stages lead to innovative and relevant solutions that meet the needs and expectations of all participants" [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Co-design originated in the 1970s from user-driven design in the USA and participatory design in Northern Europe, shifting from supplier-centred to user-centred approaches [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- 64% of studies used a pre-defined framework or model; the Double Diamond model was the most frequently cited (6 studies), followed by human-centred co-design, Boyd's framework, and Sanders & Stappers [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Co-design processes ranged from 3 to 7 stages, with 4–5 stages most common (51%); the basic archetype is needs assessment (input), collaborative design (process), and prototype development (output) [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Applying the [[mate-taxonomy|MATE taxonomy]], participants acted mostly as informants, consultants, and co-designers; few studies progressed to prototype testing, and participant engagement was predominantly low to low-medium [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Only 4 of 55 studies (7%) reported strategies to mitigate power dynamics (e.g. Indigenous research guidelines, separate sessions for clients and practitioners) [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Stakeholder participation is often extractive rather than generative — gathering information without fostering ownership and active collaboration [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Academic research often relies on qualitative methods that can lead to disengagement; creative and participatory methods are more prevalent in grey literature [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Publication surge in 2023 (34% of all studies); Oceania (primarily Australia) accounted for 38% of publications, likely influenced by the Australian National Preventive Health Strategy (2021–2030) which recommends co-design [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- 55% of studies focused on health services, 38% on health promotion, and 7% on preventive health [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- Recruitment and sampling methods were the most poorly reported quality criteria across studies [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]
- The review calls for minimum standards for co-design, standardized reporting guidelines, and clearly defined co-design principles to improve consistency and enable cross-study comparison [[raw/Exploring co-design_ a systematic review of concepts, processes, models, and frameworks used in public health research.md|Vargas et al.]]

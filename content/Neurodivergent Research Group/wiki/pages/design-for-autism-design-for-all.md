# Design for Autism, Design for All

## Summary

"When we design for autism, we design better for everyone" is a thesis advanced by [[magda-mostafa|Magda Mostafa]] arguing that autistic sensory needs, when centred in design, produce environments that benefit the full neurological spectrum — including neurotypical individuals. The thesis extends the logic of [[universal-design-public-space|universal design]]: removing barriers for the most constrained user improves access for all.

## The Claim

Mostafa acknowledges this "sounds like a very romantic notion" and that "until we have data to support that claim, a lot of people just brush it off as self-promotional." Her Venice Biennale 2023 installation was designed in part as a proof-of-concept experiment [[raw/When We Design for Autism, We Design for Everyone]].

## Pilot Evidence

The Venice Biennale 2023 escape space installation gathered visitor feedback on the calming effect of stepping into a sensory refuge within a simulated overstimulating cityscape. Preliminary results: both autistic and non-autistic visitors reported a sense of calm at almost the same levels. This is early-stage data, not peer-reviewed [[raw/When We Design for Autism, We Design for Everyone]].

See also [[sensory-refuge#Cross-Neurotype Efficacy]].

## Direct Articulation (Mostafa, 2021)

> "Autism is just a heightened manifestation of challenges that most people have about processing their environment or engaging with sensory stimulation around them. Autism can be seen as an exaggerated focus on these elements, so when you tackle the problem from that perspective you can create much friendlier, softer, respectful, and comfortable spaces for everyone." [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]]

Benefits extend beyond autistic individuals to other groups with overlapping sensory/cognitive needs: the elderly, people with dementia, immunocompromised individuals in post-COVID contexts [[raw/Autism friendly design – an interview with Magda Mostafa.md]].

## Mechanism

The argument rests on two observations:

1. **Sensory overload is not autism-specific.** Modern cities generate sensory environments driven by commodification, advertising, and social media that tax *everyone's* cognitive bandwidth and attention — not only those with diagnosed sensory differences [[sensory-decolonisation]].

2. **Sensory regulation needs exist on a spectrum.** When sensory information reaches a threshold, "there's a certain point where you can't process anything anymore" — a phenomenological reality for both autistic and neurotypical people, differing in threshold rather than kind [[raw/When We Design for Autism, We Design for Everyone]].

## Relation to Universal Design

This thesis parallels the curb-cut effect in physical accessibility: solutions designed for wheelchair users (dropped kerbs, ramps) prove useful to parents with strollers, delivery workers, cyclists, and elderly people. Mostafa's analogy: we wouldn't "throw in a flight of steps without an elevator or ramp — so why are we throwing in this overstimulation that's just as potentially disabling?" ([[sensory-decolonisation]])

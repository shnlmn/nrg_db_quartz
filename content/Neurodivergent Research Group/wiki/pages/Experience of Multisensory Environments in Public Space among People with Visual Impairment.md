# Experience of Multisensory Environments in Public Space among People with Visual Impairment

**Full title:** Experience of Multisensory Environments in Public Space among People with Visual Impairment

**Authors:** Gavin R. Jenkins, Hon K. Yuen, Laura K. Vogtle
**Journal:** International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health
**Year:** 2015  
**DOI:** 10.3390/ijerph120808644
**Pages:** 8644–8657

## Summary

Qualitative study exploring how sensory characteristics in the built environment support or hinder public space access for people with visual impairment. Analyzed written survey responses from 48 respondents with visual impairment recruited through National Federation of the Blind chapters across 34 US states.

## Key findings

- **Three main themes** emerged: [[sensory-barriers-navigation|Barriers]], [[auditory-navigation-cues#supporters|Supporters]], and [[context-dependent-sensory-effectiveness|Context-Dependence]]

- **Barriers to participation:** Population-specific design (spaces built without considering non-visual users), extreme sensory backgrounds (masking noise, echo, or absent cues), uneven ground surfaces and objects, and inconsistent lighting

- **Sensory supporters:** Audible cues and echoes, olfactory landmarks, tactile ground surface texture, temperature/airflow, and landmark sounds

- **Context matters:** Same sensory cue can be helpful or hindering depending on proximity, intensity, the activity being performed, and the individual's residual vision

- **Universal design approach:** Recommendations for architects, designers, and occupational therapists to embed functional sensory cues in built environments without stigmatizing particular groups

## Key claims

- People with visual impairment decode multiple sensory streams simultaneously to navigate and participate in public spaces [[sensory-characteristics-visual-impairment]]

- The "accessibility problem" is not physical inaccessibility per se, but lack of information sources that can be accessed without vision [[visual-impairment-public-space-access]]

- [[occupational-therapy-visual-impairment|Occupational therapy has a pivotal role]] in bridging the gap between accessibility initiatives and full participation

- Sensory cue effectiveness is [[context-dependent-sensory-effectiveness|highly context-dependent]], requiring designers to consider proximity, intensity, activity type, and individual variability

- [[universal-design-public-space|Universal design principles]] allow public spaces to benefit all users—including those with sensory disabilities—without visible stigmatization

## Methodology

- Cross-sectional survey design
- 451 direct invitations sent via NFB chapter presidents
- 85 responses (18.8% response rate) from 34 US states
- 48 respondents answered one or both open-ended questions
- Thematic content analysis using constant comparative method
- Data triangulation via investigator consensus (3 independent coders)

## Open questions

- How do severity of vision loss and specific diagnoses affect sensory cue utilization? (Authors note data did not capture this)
- Generalizability: Results reflect people with visual impairment who have access to technology; would findings differ for those without?
- How can architects practically balance competing sensory needs of different users?

# Sensory Wayfinding

## Summary

Sensory wayfinding is the practice of navigating public and urban spaces by attending to sensory intensity gradients — identifying routes and positions that minimise sensory overload while preserving access to desired destinations. It frames avoidance of overstimulating environments not as a coping limitation but as a navigation problem amenable to spatial analysis.

## Core Tension

Avoidance of sensory overload carries a direct cost: **avoidance is loss of opportunity** (for education, earning, company, and leisure). This makes the optimisation of benefit against discomfort a practical necessity, not merely a preference. Sensory wayfinding addresses this by treating high-intensity zones as terrain to route around rather than barriers requiring withdrawal.

## Gradient as Key Feature

A **gradient** of motion intensity (or more broadly, sensory intensity) is the critical spatial feature enabling sensory wayfinding:

- Gradation provides a choice of comfort level — individuals can migrate into the intensity and speed that suits their needs, which varies between people and within individuals across time and mood.
- A **flat** intensity map (uniform footfall throughout, e.g., a packed marketplace or airport check-in queue) provides no sensory refuge or decompression zone.
- Some retail environments deliberately flatten intensity to maximise footfall per square metre — a design decision hostile to sensory wayfinding.

## Imaginary Roads Metaphor

[[stuart-neilson|Stuart Neilson]]'s semimaginary terrain (see [[motion-intensity-heatmap]]) makes the wayfinding problem visible: high-intensity areas form ridge lines dividing the sensory landscape. Flight lines into refuge follow the equivalent of river channels — low-intensity paths running perpendicular (downhill) from busy zones. But these refuge spaces may be disconnected from each other and from desired destinations.

The imaginary-road solution: routes that run **parallel** to high-intensity paths, connecting refuges and both sides of intensity ridges, merging into the flow at gentle angles (avoiding perpendicular conflict), and crossing ridges at minimum-gradient passes. This minimises sensory cost while preserving urban connectivity.

## ASPECTSS Domains

Neilson maps sensory wayfinding directly onto six of the [[aspectss-design-index|ASPECTSS®]] domains:
- **Acoustics** — managing auditory intensity along routes
- **Spatial Sequencing** — ordering of spaces to manage sensory transition
- **Escape Space** — designated low-intensity areas accessible from high-intensity zones
- **Compartmentalization** — separating zones of differing intensity
- **Transitions** — mediating between high- and low-stimulation environments
- **Sensory Zoning** — differentiated areas offering varied sensory affordances

## Typologies Over Individual Spaces

Neilson emphasises interest in **typologies** of public space (gradient patterns, ridge structures, refuge morphology) over individual spatial analyses — because the same spatial problems (high-intensity ridges disconnecting refuge from destination) recur across global city contexts.

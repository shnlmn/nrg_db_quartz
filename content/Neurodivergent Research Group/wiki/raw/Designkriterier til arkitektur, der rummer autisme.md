The world is not designed for people with neuromiscellaneous disorders such as autism. To offset the difference, architect and researcher Magda Mostafa, with Autism Aspectss, have set up seven design criteria that can promote architecture that accommodate people with autism and at the same time contributes to well-being among all.

[About 1% of the population is on the autism spectrum](https://psykiatrifonden.dk/diagnoser/autisme). For children, the number is a little higher. Autism is a developmental disorder that has an impact on the way the world is perceived. People with autism see, hear and feel the world differently than other people, but are expected to be able to navigate a world that is not immediately designed for them.

Autism is called a spectrum disorder because it covers a wide field of disturbances that can affect people with autism in different ways. One of the characteristics that many with autism share is that they are more sensitive to sense impressions. They can have a hard time sorting in sense impressions, which can thus feel like very dominant and difficult to abstract from.

It can make everyday situations like moving through a noisy room or participating in joint education, situations where a person with autism is easily overstimulated and thus cannot participate on an equal footing with others.

#### Seven design criteria from an autistic lens

Based on his research, the Egyptian architect and researcher Magda Mostafa in 2013 formulated seven design criteria in the Autism ASPECTSS Design Index, which can help architects and designers understand the autistic challenges.

The seven design criteria in Autism Aspects are, among other things, about creating logic in space, designing controlled acoustics and designing spaces that allow you to escape all unnecessary sense impressions. In this way, the intention is to create architecture that supports people with autism's well-being.

Looking at the seven criteria in the index, it becomes clear: If we design on the basis of a goal of creating well-being for people with autism, we create space that is weird and safe for everyone. Because we all benefit from having proper space, being able to see somewhere and staying places with good sound conditions.

**What is neurodiversity?** ⁇

It is easy to see that our bodies are not the same. Swearing is to spot that our brains are also set differently. It was Australian sociologist Judy Singer, who first came up with the concept of ‘neurodiversity ’, which basically covers the variation between our brains.

Neurodiversity is thus a unifying term for all brains – those with atypical characteristics such as diagnosis or disorders and the so-called ‘neurotypical ’. Living with a neurodivergent brain is not always a disadvantage – especially if one's social and physical environment fits it.

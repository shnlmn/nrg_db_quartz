### DUBLIN CITY UNIVERSITY (DCU)

Dublin City University (DCU) was designated in 2018 as the world's first autism-friendly university. It is committed to supporting and celebrating all staff and students who are on the autism spectrum.

DCU works closely with global best practices and has committed to adapting the environment, raising awareness, acceptance and building initiatives to make it as easy as possible for autistic community members to participate fully in all aspects of university life. The intent of this Autism Friendly University Design Guide is to provide the requisite built environment infrastructure to realize their goals.

## DESIGN THINKING – THE AUTISTIC PERSPECTIVE

### THINKING DESIGN TOOLS

Tools specific to the autistic community for use during workshops specifically with and for the autistic community.

The design thinking tools and cues can illustrate ideas and problem spaces in the design thinking workshop, and help in organizing material to illustrate concepts.

**Key Design Thinking Principles:**

**Visual over Verbal**
- Help images and cues, prompts visual can illustrate ideas and problem spaces in the design thinking workshop
- Visual material can be particularly helpful in orienting, connecting concepts
- Typically design thinking sessions may quickly become visually stimulating-over and chaotic, which can be overwhelming for the autistic participant
- Efforts were made to create clear grids of space where ideas specific to be shared were created for each new portion of the workshop
- Separate "fresh starts" and boards

**Order over Stimulation**
- Design thinking sessions typically flow with layering and multiple ideas
- For autistic participants, this can quickly become overwhelming and chaotic visually
- Efforts were made to create clear grids of space where specific ideas were to be shared
- Separate boards and "fresh starts" were created for each new portion of the workshop

**Personal over Social Broadly**
- Refinement and organization was carried out to create participation barriers for autistic individuals
- The total of 15 participants were split into groups of 5 with one moderator
- This allowed more personal connection among participants and a more manageable social environment

**Autistic Social Space over Neurotypical Social Space**
- The organization of participants was balanced to allow comfortable and stigma-free sharing of autistic voice as the "expert voice"
- Groupings were curated to include representation from all stakeholders, with a focus on the autistic user as expert perspective

**Planning Ahead and Social Familiarization**
- Detailed agenda and participant bios were shared ahead of the workshop
- A video tutorial illustrating how to use the virtual board was shared
- A contact person for technical support was provided
- Time was allocated in the agenda to ensure everyone was comfortable using the platform
- Everyone was familiar with one another and their role in the workshop

**Timing and Scheduling is Key**
- The shared agenda was organized with granularity to the 5-minute intervals
- Each activity had clear start and stop times with clear times for breaks
- Activities themselves were timed with a visual timer that everyone could see
- This practice is common in Design Thinking and very supportive from the autistic perspective

**Predictability and Preparedness**
- As a general concept, in the planning and process of the workshop, objectives were always stated clearly
- The agenda was always visible and often recapped
- Expectations were always clear

Although some design thinking methods may remain open-ended to encourage lack of conformity of ideas, this lack of structure and expectation may be difficult for autistic users, so expectations are shared and clarity provided for new ideas proposed.

## EPILOGUE

Higher education campuses have throughout our modern history been the sites of contemplation, scientific discovery, and shifting paradigm debate. There is no doubt that such progress was not, nor will it be possible without different ways of thinking. The thinking that diversity brings to the table is innovative and unique. It is the diversity that provides the thinking of ways and kinds of thinking that the neurodiversity brings.

Without diversity, the lenses through which we can observe the world and the problems we approach and the solutions we develop will not be challenged to make often field-shifting discoveries. But our knowledge will stagnate and our academic minds will not grow, adapt and develop to create the solutions needed for our unknown future.

If such diversity is not supported by a holistic ecosystem of support, facilitation, and accommodation, such diversity cannot be productive and effective.

DCU has taken strides towards creating this ecosystem of academic, social and operational support. With this guide it has expanded those supports to include the environment built. Our hope is that this guide will help complete the holistic landscape for diversity to flourish.

We hope that this guide will not only be included into, but contribute to the paradigm shifting knowledge production needed across higher education globally, so that this guide provides a template to be scaled up to cities and neighborhoods, across European nations and the world, for an autism friendly world, where diversity can flourish everywhere.

Introduction 
In adult services, assessment for Autism, ADHD or other neurodevelopmental conditions should include 
assessment of sensory preferences. Associated information about fuller neurodevelopmental assessment can 
be found in the following resource: https://www.thirdspace.scot/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/NAIT-

Sensory Preferences 
Sensory preferences have been described in relation to sound, vision, taste, touch, and smell. They also include 
vestibular (movement and balance), proprioception (body awareness) or interoception (how our body feels 
inside).  Differences can include hypersensitivity, hyposensitivity, and general sensory overload.  
 
Diagnostic criteria relevant to sensory preferences 
Sensory reactivity is one of the core diagnostic criteria for ASD. A mismatch between sensory preferences and 
the physical and social environments in every day life have been associated with poorer outcomes and distress. 
 
Sensory differences are also important in diagnostic criteria for ADHD, for example in people who are 
movement seeking, fidgety and for whom the sensory environment affects regulation, attention focus and 
concentration. 
 
DSM 5 and ICD 11 diagnostic criteria use deficit focussed language and as clinicians we can reframe the 
language and mindsets we apply when talking with and writing or thinking about neurodivergent people. Some 
examples are here:

Sensory Assessment 
Assessment can include direct observation, environmental assessment, and self or proxy reports. There are 
benefits and limitations in all available assessment tools, and it is recommended that a variety of assessment 
approaches are used. Observations in real world situations may support clinicians to make decisions regarding 
environmental accommodations or adaptations. Interventions should be focussed on enhancing daily 
functioning and participation in everyday life, at home, at work, in the community and in education. 
 
Occupational Therapists are key members of the team and have expertise in this area, to inform, teach and 
offer consultation. However, assessment of sensory preferences can be done through self-assessment or by 
other team members with the right knowledge and skills.

Supporting sensory needs 
Understanding sensory preferences can support an individual to anticipate situations where they may feel 
overwhelmed. These preferences can also be used to develop helpful strategies to assist the person to manage 
their needs by reducing stimuli they find stressful or to participate in activities that make them feel calmer. 
Some examples include: 
 
Calming strategies: pressure blanket, sucking a sweet, going for a walk, massage, lavender scent, take a break, 
subdued lighting, headphones, use stairs instead of lift/escalator, sunglasses, close your eyes for a minute. 
 
Alerting Strategies: jogging/running, jumping jacks/ push ups, eating crunchy food, drinking cold drinks, play 
music, move around, use perfume, work in a busy office, bright lighting, group activities.

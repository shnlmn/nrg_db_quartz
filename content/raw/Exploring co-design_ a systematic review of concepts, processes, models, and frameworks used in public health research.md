## Abstract

Background

Co-design has emerged as a preferred collaborative approach among public health practitioners and researchers to increase the potential for effective design, implementation, and dissemination of innovations in practice. However, there is a lack of consistency in its application and reporting, which limits co-design theory building and best practice. This systematic review aimed to examine the peer-reviewed literature reporting on the use of co-design as a process in public health, synthesizing co-design definitions, processes, models or frameworks, and participant involvement.

Methods

Following the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, a systematic review was conducted. MEDLINE Complete, Global Health, and CINAHL databases were searched in April 2024.

Results

Fifty-five articles on co-design in public health published between 2016 and 2024 were included, noting a publication surge in 2023. Most studies were from Oceania, especially Australia. While 44% lacked a co-design definition, 56% highlighted its collaborative, inclusive nature. Over half (64%) used specific frameworks with a 4–5 stage process. Participant roles varied from informants to co-designers, with many studies not developing prototypes for testing.

Conclusions

A more transparent and systematic application of co-design principles, guided by clearer frameworks and reporting standards, could improve the consistency and effectiveness of co-design in public health.

## Introduction

To improve the uptake, utility, and outcomes of public health innovations (e.g. initiatives, tools, or services), researchers, practitioners, and policymakers (the stakeholders) actively seek ways to involve diverse stakeholders in the design and decision-making processes.<sup>1</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>2</sup> Co-design has emerged as a preferred collaborative approach among stakeholders to increase the potential for effective design, implementation and dissemination of innovations in practice.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>4</sup> It is argued that the joint development of new ideas and subsequent enhanced communication and collaboration between researchers and non-researchers provide more context-specific initiatives, services, and more effective tools for improving public health.<sup>2</sup>

Co-design emerged in the 1970s from user-driven design in the USA and participatory design in Northern Europe, shifting from supplier-centred to user-centred approaches.<sup>5</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>6</sup> It builds on participatory and user-centred design principles <sup>7</sup> and has influenced various fields, including architecture, education, health services, social policy, and technology.<sup>5</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>6</sup> Despite its collaborative nature, which values the expertise and lived experience of participants, co-design is sometimes practised without adhering to rigorous processes.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>8</sup> This raises concerns about the authenticity and equity of collaboration, making the benefits to the groups served less clear.<sup>4</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>8</sup>

Authors and practitioners have defined the concept of co-design in diverse ways.<sup>3</sup> Co-design can be defined as the process of active collaboration between stakeholders in designing solutions to a prespecified problem.<sup>2</sup> In health research, stakeholders include service users, community representatives, and various government entities.<sup>9</sup> The principle of co-design practice emphasizes that these contributions are valuable, expert, and essential to the process’s success.<sup>5</sup> Incorporating prototype development with iterative quality improvement cycles enhances stakeholder engagement, actively acknowledges their contributions, and improves final outcomes.<sup>10</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>11</sup> We understand co-design as a process of joint inquiry and imagination, as stated by Steen, where stakeholders are empowered to participate in inquiry (explore, discuss, and define a problem) and imagination (explore, develop, and evaluate possible solutions) through a series of interconnected and iterative phases.<sup>12</sup>

This systematic review aims to examine the peer-reviewed literature reporting on the use of co-design as a process in public health, synthesizing co-design definitions, processes, models and frameworks, and participant involvement. Specifically, this review addresses the following research questions:

How has co-design been defined in public health research?

What co-design processes, models, and frameworks have been used in public health co-design research?

How do stakeholders participate in public health co-design research according to the methodology, agent of change, training, and engagement taxonomy of participatory design?

This approach was considered adequate for synthesizing existing co-design stepped processes and clarifying their application in public health. This understanding will guide future practices, enhance reporting consistency, and support the creation of more effective and inclusive co-design public health initiatives. The protocol was registered on Prospero (registration no. CRD42021226566), with no major changes made to the original version.

### Information sources

The search strategy was developed with a qualified librarian and informed by previous systematic reviews on co-design as a research process ([Supplementary Table S2](https://oup.silverchair-cdn.com/oup/backfile/Content_public/Journal/jpubhealth/47/4/10.1093_pubmed_fdaf084/1/additional_material_fdaf084.docx?Expires=1779223500&Signature=js~44RyvSWDhx2ZLnN3XH1M5JGxqd74bdwFY-vZqiVHUimj646366TMsklnxrHrj8X2Dzspy4jnyRC0CaOe0AWi3-piOjz47NykPLOkmvZo68vRzDrokrxzCk8q5OaLqceV5PpsBDkHBh6jJELb91rFk6u-pN3GH8AVANaoQqKxKpk8k8seWZMRUMdqRY2VHLMqhOJvPVvyyO1j1EzQzelz71j~j7gP2SyK7h5WJxEt84YHqm8e2eYV2NbvkgpOSTQc-kRJ1xT9aT8V5p~gL26gzXutkYNT9qeWM-15XjPxYKXZO1z6jtKKENCU29UouTkZtwEw4yN6jiYJMVbmWKA__&Key-Pair-Id=APKAIE5G5CRDK6RD3PGA)). Searches were conducted in MEDLINE Complete, Global Health, and CINAHL (via Ebsco Host), limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English, and published from the inception of each database to 23 April 2024. A hand search of reference lists from included studies was also conducted to identify additional relevant articles.

### Study selection

We included studies that:

1. Reported an underpinning theoretical approach for co-designed public health research (e.g. participatory action research, experience-based (co) design, or co-design).
2. Described a process where participants had active involvement in developing public health outputs (e.g. policy, tools, health promotion materials or strategies, or service delivery improvement).

Active involvement was defined as explicit, well-documented participation essential to the research process, with the potential for participants to influence outcomes.<sup>3</sup> Essential to active involvement is the potential for participants to influence the process. Therefore, studies were excluded if they:

1. Used co-design as a participatory method or data collection activity (e.g. workshops, needs assessment, and formative research) without any description of the co-design process that these activities informed.
2. Focused on the dissemination or translation of research findings without describing the co-design process (e.g. evaluation of a co-designed output).
3. Reviews, conference abstracts, letters to the editor, and protocols were ineligible. The grey literature was not included in this review.

### Data collection process

Search results were imported into Endnote X9 for duplicate removal. The remaining citations were then transferred to Covidence for screening, data extraction, and quality assessment. Two researchers from the screening team independently performed primary and secondary screenings of the results. They assessed titles and abstracts in the primary screening, followed by a detailed review of full texts for eligibility criteria in the secondary screening. Any discrepancies between reviewers were discussed until agreement was reached.

A data extraction schema, developed in consultation with all authors, was used to extract data from each article. Thirty-five per cent of articles (*n* = 20) were independently extracted and assessed by a second author. All discrepancies were discussed until a mutually agreed-upon solution was reached. Extracted data were exported from Covidence to Microsoft Excel (2018).

### Synthesis methods

A numerical summary and narrative description of the findings were created. Data were descriptively analysed by year of publication, country, public health area, including a co-design definition, outputs generated, number of steps or stages in the co-design process, and if a pre-defined process was used (considered when authors included a citation for the process). Under the stated research questions, co-design definitions were cross-checked against the citing reference to capture the original wording and synthesized as an overarching definition.

The co-designed process details (including participants’ characteristics, steps, and methods for each stage or step) were inductively analysed in two steps. First, data were coded according to the individuals who participated in the co-design process, the steps or stages in which they were involved, and the data collection method used. This was followed by a deductive analysis against the agent of change, training, and engagement (MATE) taxonomy of participatory design. The MATE taxonomy conceptualizes ‘agent of change’ under six key roles a participant may take at various project stages, including informant, consultant, co-designer, tester, leader, and trainee.

The MATE taxonomy <sup>15</sup> does not intend to evaluate participatory design outcomes; instead, it was designed to understand and classify participatory design applications according to methods more likely to facilitate more meaningful participant engagement (defined as the level of user involvement in the design process). It categorizes engagement on a low-medium-high continuum comprising three factors: agency, input, and empowerment. Methods not explicitly described in the MATE taxonomy (e.g. meetings and discussion sessions) were categorized as a ‘group dynamic’ with a medium level of engagement. Depending on the contribution of participants, these methods were considered informants or co-designers. This taxonomy helped us better understand and classify the level of participant involvement and the potential for meaningful engagement.

### Quality assessment

The Quality Assessment Tool for Studies with Diverse Designs (QuADS) was used to assess the methodological rigour and trustworthiness of the included studies given the heterogeneity of the evidence base. The QuADS tool includes 13 items, each rated on a scale from 0 to 3, and assesses various aspects of study quality. This tool does not specify a cut-off score for high or low quality; instead, individual scores for each criterion were considered in the analysis to indicate the comprehensiveness of reporting across studies. No articles were excluded based on quality assessment, as this review aimed to identify available evidence to answer our research questions.

### Co-design definitions

Forty-four per cent (*n* = 24) of selected articles did not define co-design. The remaining 56% (*n* = 31) included definitions to justify the use of a co-design approach. With most referring to co-design (*n* = 22),<sup>7</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>15–19</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>22</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>23</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>37–50</sup> while others used terms like human-centred design (*n* = 3),<sup>22</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>25</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>66</sup> user-centred design (*n* = 2),<sup>15</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>16</sup> experience-based co-design (*n* = 2),<sup>31</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>41</sup> or co-creation (*n* = 2).<sup>27</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>52</sup> Across 24 different definitions, co-design was described as a collaborative and inclusive design approach, or a method for co-creation, involving the participation of diverse stakeholders in the design process to address a predefined problem. These definitions describe that participation can occur in various degrees of intensity and stages to ensure that the end product meets the needs and expectations of all involved parties. Multiple studies emphasized the involvement of end users (e.g. patients, health workers, and customers). These reports highlighted that co-design should lead to innovative solutions (e.g. services or products) that are relevant and acceptable to those participating in the process <sup>2</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>6</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>7</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>12</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>27</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>68–82</sup> ([Supplementary Table S4](https://oup.silverchair-cdn.com/oup/backfile/Content_public/Journal/jpubhealth/47/4/10.1093_pubmed_fdaf084/1/additional_material_fdaf084.docx?Expires=1779223500&Signature=js~44RyvSWDhx2ZLnN3XH1M5JGxqd74bdwFY-vZqiVHUimj646366TMsklnxrHrj8X2Dzspy4jnyRC0CaOe0AWi3-piOjz47NykPLOkmvZo68vRzDrokrxzCk8q5OaLqceV5PpsBDkHBh6jJELb91rFk6u-pN3GH8AVANaoQqKxKpk8k8seWZMRUMdqRY2VHLMqhOJvPVvyyO1j1EzQzelz71j~j7gP2SyK7h5WJxEt84YHqm8e2eYV2NbvkgpOSTQc-kRJ1xT9aT8V5p~gL26gzXutkYNT9qeWM-15XjPxYKXZO1z6jtKKENCU29UouTkZtwEw4yN6jiYJMVbmWKA__&Key-Pair-Id=APKAIE5G5CRDK6RD3PGA)).

### MATE taxonomy application

The application of the MATE taxonomy revealed a range of roles for participants and researchers in the co-design applications among the included studies. Participants acted mostly as informants, consultants, and co-designers, depending on the methods applied. Few studies progressed to a prototype stage where participants acted as testers. Only one study,<sup>43</sup> considered training as an influential part of the prototype development phase, where students were trained to use a heart rate monitor and differentiate heart rate zones, enabling them to design high-intensity interval training routines. The extent of participant engagement ranged from low to low-medium, across most co-design steps, depending on the level of agency, input, and empowerment afforded by the methods. For example, needs assessment or identification of needs was typically conducted through a literature review or a combination of literature review and interviews and/or focus groups, resulting in low to medium levels of participant engagement. Some co-design processes relied primarily on low-engagement methods (e.g. interviews, surveys) throughout the process. In contrast, others applied low-middle levels of participant engagement (e.g. literature review and/or interviews and focus groups) Table 2.

## Discussion

### Main finding of this study

This systematic review synthesized the current literature on co-design as a process in public health, identifying 55 articles published between 2016 and 2024. The findings highlight the potential of co-design to enhance the development and implementation of public health interventions, tools, services, or content by promoting collaboration, inclusivity, and user-centredness. A key strength of this review is the use of the MATE taxonomy to categorize levels of participation in co-design applications. This framework facilitated a deeper understanding of stakeholder roles and engagement methods, highlighting potential best practices in co-design based on participant input in the final prototype. Unlike previous reviews that focused solely on methods,<sup>83</sup> this is the first review to expand on the stakeholder extent and level of participation from the methods selected in the co-design process. Co-design has been widely discussed across various contexts, revealing a rich collection of diverse definitions and interpretations that often lead to inconsistencies, challenging cross-study comparisons and hindering theoretical development.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>4</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>83</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup> Defining key concepts is essential, as it establishes a foundation relevant to research and clarifies how these ideas connect to the broader field.<sup>85</sup> This definition inconsistency contributes to knowledge fragmentation and impedes the systematic advancement of co-design theory and practice, highlighting the need for clearer frameworks and terminologies in future research.<sup>2</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>86</sup>

We found an increased number of publications from Australia, which may be related to the active use of the term ‘co-design’ in government public health strategies. For example, the Australian National Preventive Health Strategy (2021–2030) <sup>87</sup> suggests prioritizing co-design approaches to develop, deliver, and evaluate services to achieve greater outcomes. However, this strategy does not specify a design framework, principles, or best practice guidelines, highlighting an opportunity for further standardization in co-design applications. Studies investigating co-design methods, definitions, and approaches <sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>4</sup> report that the limited transparency in study methodology makes cross-study comparisons and evaluations of co-design’s effectiveness difficult.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>48</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>88</sup> For example, only 4 (7%) included studies reported strategies to mitigate power dynamics (i.e. incorporation of Indigenous guidelines for health research,<sup>29</sup> or running separate sessions for clients/patients and health practitioners <sup>1</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>18</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>63</sup>). However, it is unclear whether these strategies had a significant influence on the outcomes compared to other studies. A more systematic application of co-design processes and applicable guiding principles is essential to facilitate better planning, execution, and evaluation of initiatives.<sup>83</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>87</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>89</sup> Additionally, it could help to make comparisons and establish better ways to manage diverse stakeholder expectations, balance power dynamics, and ensure sufficient resources and time for meaningful participation.<sup>84</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>88</sup>

There are widespread and well-known generic models for a systematic design.<sup>90</sup> Dubberly (2004) <sup>90</sup> collated more than one hundred design models seeking to enable designers to understand, connect with, and motivate stakeholders to share their lived experiences in designing an end product. While the models presented by Dubberly do not consider co-design as such or its application in public health research, there are similarities in the processes, models, and frameworks reported in our results. A clear example is Banathy’s <sup>90</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>, p.24</sup> dynamics of divergence and convergence model, which may have influenced the Double Diamond model, as both follow an iterative design of repeated divergence and convergence processes, analysis, and synthesis.<sup>28</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>36</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>43</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>48</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>57</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>67</sup>

Our results (Fig. 2) indicate that a three-stage co-design process has a similar basic Dubberly’s design archetype <sup>90</sup> —needs assessment (input), a collaborative design process (black box), and prototype development (output), with additional stages reflecting researchers’ attention oscillating between the analysis and synthesis of results to report back to stakeholders or ensure and validate the identification of solutions. However, the ambiguity in defining stakeholder engagement creates doubts about authentic empowerment.<sup>10</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>14</sup> Our findings suggest that a more transparent and systematic application of co-design principles, guided by more explicit frameworks and reporting standards, could improve the consistency and effectiveness of co-design in public health. To put these findings into practice, researchers, policymakers, and public health practitioners should implement minimum standards for co-design, guided by systematic incorporation and clearly defined co-design principles. As a result, more consistent co-design application and integration of user feedback could lead to effectively meeting community needs.

### What this study adds

This review highlights a significant issue in co-design practices within public health: the variability in stakeholder participation, often criticized for being extractive rather than generative.<sup>4</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>8</sup> This extractive approach gathers information from stakeholders without fostering their ownership and engagement through active collaboration.<sup>18</sup> Our results indicate that the level of participant engagement is influenced by the extent of agency and empowerment granted to stakeholders through the chosen methods. A recent systematic inventory of methods used in co-creation (fundamentally understood as design co-creation <sup>2</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>91</sup>) reveals that creative and participatory methods are more prevalent in grey literature. In contrast, academic research often relies on qualitative methods, which can lead to disengagement and insufficient reassessment of power dynamics. Our results suggest that to explore the full potential of co-design towards a more generative process where a higher level of stakeholder engagement is enabled, research methods should be directed towards increasing stakeholder autonomy, contribution, and empowerment, and lessons from grey literature could be considered.<sup>92</sup>

Our findings align with previous studies highlighting the diversity and inconsistency of co-design definitions, limiting cross-study comparisons and theoretical development.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>4</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>83</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup> Although many authors have attempted to define co-design, there is no agreement on a single co-design definition. Using the considerations of the 31 studies (56%) that included a definition, we defined co-design as a collaborative and inclusive design approach that actively involves diverse stakeholders in the design process to address a specific problem, ensuring that varying degrees of participation throughout different stages lead to innovative and relevant solutions that meet the needs and expectations of all participants. This definition extends our pre-conception of co-design from Vargas et al.<sup>2</sup> by emphasizing inclusivity, various degrees of participation, and the significance of addressing stakeholder needs in the design process. These additional considerations could be supported by clearly defined principles for co-design to inform theory and practice.<sup>21</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>93–97</sup>

The inconsistent reporting of co-design processes and participant engagement poses a significant challenge, as inadequate documentation hinders the development of co-design principles and limits reproducibility and cross-setting applications.<sup>16</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup> Our findings suggest that a more transparent and systematic application of co-design principles, guided by clearer frameworks, defined principles and reporting standards, could improve the consistency and effectiveness of co-design in public health. Researchers advocate for more transparent reporting, including detailed descriptions of methods and stakeholder involvement.<sup>3</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>83</sup><sup>,</sup><sup>84</sup> While the inconsistency might call for developing a standard reporting or expanding on an existing one, examples of such may include the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement,<sup>98</sup> Kiss et al.<sup>84</sup> offer recommendations to enhance co-design methodology reporting. Our results contribute to identifying common co-design stages and activities across the evidence, which could help inform the development of such co-design reporting guidelines. Better reporting standards, standard co-design principles and guidelines can ensure co-design is done in the most meaningful and impactful way.

### Limitations of this study

A limitation of this systematic review is its focus on peer-reviewed literature, which may have missed better-documented co-design examples from the grey literature. However, some articles did incorporate the grey literature co-design processes into their study design. Additionally, the review concentrated on articles that specifically reported co-design methodologies, potentially overlooking relevant research that employed less structured approaches. Expanding the scope of ‘co-design approaches’ may identify broader studies (i.e. group model building as a co-design approach). However, this review builds on the earlier work of Vargas et al.<sup>2</sup> where a range of terms often used have been applied.

## Conclusion

This review emphasizes the potential of co-design in public health for enhancing initiatives, tools, and services through collaboration, inclusivity, and a user-centred approach to enhance initiatives through collaboration and user-centred approaches. However, challenges such as inconsistent definitions and varying stakeholder engagement underscore the need for a clear framework, defined principles, and standardized reporting. Incorporating creative, participatory methods, and insights from non-academic sources is essential to improve the co-design process and foster innovative practices within the community. Future research should focus on refining co-design principles and establishing a standardized framework to guide its application in public health.
